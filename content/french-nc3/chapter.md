Against 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 the Exchange (3.exd5) asks nothing of Black and gives White an equal, symmetrical
position. 3.Nc3 is the replacement with teeth. The knight defends e4 and lets Black decide: the Winawer
pin (3...Bb4), the Classical 3...Nf6, or the capture 3...dxe4. Each of the three has a sharp White answer
and a pawn structure that White understands better than Black.

The repertoire has three parts. Against the **Winawer** White plays 4.e5 c5 5.a3 Bxc3+ 6.bxc3 and then 7.Qg4,
the poisoned pawn. Against the **Classical** White plays the Steinitz, 4.e5 Nfd7 5.f4, which is the same
structure the Fantasy chapter already teaches. Against the **Rubinstein** (3...dxe4 4.Nxe4) White gets a
free, harmonious game, and 4...Nd7 5.Nf3 Ngf6 6.Nxf6+ Nxf6 is met by 7.c3 or 7.Bd3.

Be plain about how well sourced it is. There is no popular Lichess study written for White against 3.Nc3: the
big French studies are Black repertoires. The chapter is built from a 40-like White repertoire study
(Beat The French), the Black-side lines of GM Giri and GM Berg (inside a 22-like study), the outlines of
Bologan's DVD and Ponomariov's White course, Wikibooks, and a few smaller studies. The Winawer main lines and
the Steinitz are reasonably well covered; the Rubinstein is thinner and **7.c3 is a single-source draft**; the Fort Knox and the rare
third moves are drafts. Where no moves were found, the line says so.

## Choosing between the three branches
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3
arrows: Gc3e4 Gc3d5 Rf8b4 Rg8f6
squares: Yd5 Ye4

3.Nc3 is the principled move: e4 is defended without blocking the c-pawn, and the pin or the capture is
Black's problem, not White's. What each branch asks:

- **The Winawer** is the most theoretical and the sharpest. The poisoned pawn lines have to be known to
  about move 12, but they give a game with real winning chances for White and a clear plan.
- **The Steinitz** is the middle road. One structure (the chain e5-f4, an attack on the kingside, long
  castling) covers all of Black's tries, and the theory is narrower than in the Winawer.
- **The Rubinstein** is the quiet branch: Black has no bad bishop and no break to prepare. White gets a free
  centre, but there is no forced advantage, so the plan matters more than the moves.

4.Bg5 (Burn and McCutcheon) is not in the repertoire. The Black-side study "The Aggressive French" shows
Black with an easy game after 4...dxe4, Wikipedia lists three different answers (Classical, Burn and McCutcheon),
and the move leads to more theory than 4.e5. The Bologan DVD and Ponomariov's course also choose 4.e5.

## The Winawer structure after bxc3
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Bb4 4. e5 c5 5. a3 Bxc3+ 6. bxc3
arrows: Gc1h6 Gd1g4 Rc5d4 Rc8a6
squares: Rg7 Rc3 Yd4

The exchange gave White a weak pair of c-pawns and gave Black a problem on the other wing. Both sides' plans
follow from that.

- **The dark squares.** With the dark-squared bishop gone, g7, h6 and f6 are guarded by no piece, and White's
  own dark-squared bishop has no opponent. Qg4 against g7, Bh6 and h4-h5 are the attacking ideas. This is the
  whole point of the line.
- **The doubled pawns.** c3 and c2 are weak, and the b-file is half open. But the pawns also make a chain
  c3-d4-e5 that holds the centre, and Wikibooks notes that Black's compensation is a more agile queenside
  against White's jammed centre. White should keep the chain, not break it with cxd4.
- **The bishop pair.** White has two bishops against knights, and Black's c8-bishop is the French bad bishop,
  stuck behind e6. Black's most frequent plan is to trade it by ...b6, ...Bd7 and ...Ba4 or ...Ba6 (Giri's
  chapters against 7.Nf3 and 7.a4 are built around it), and White's light-squared bishop on d3 and the
  pawn on a4 are the ways to get in its way.
- **Black's play.** ...cxd4 cxd4 Qc3+ is the threat that keeps the king from ever being comfortable. ...Qa5 and
  ...Qc7 put pressure on c3 and e5, and ...f6 is the break against the e5 pawn.

## Qg4: the decision tree
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Bb4 4. e5 c5 5. a3 Bxc3+ 6. bxc3 Ne7 7. Qg4
arrows: Gg4g7 Rc5d4 Re7f5
squares: Rg7

7.Qg4 attacks g7 at once, and nothing but a sacrifice or a concession will defend it. Black's choices, with the
repertoire answer to each:

- **7...Qc7**, the poisoned pawn. 8.Qxg7 Rg8 9.Qxh7 cxd4 10.Ne2 is the main line, and 8.Bd3 is the second
  weapon (both in file 1).
- **7...cxd4**, Berg's move order. After 8.Qxg7 it transposes; BTF's version has 11.Rb1.
- **7...O-O**, the most played. 8.Bd3 and the pieces come into play against the king (file 2).
- **7...Kf8** gives up castling; BTF's 8.Qd1 and h4-h5 is the plan (file 2).
- **7...Nf5** guards g7 with the knight; 8.Bd3 h5 9.Qd1 is the answer (file 2).

The lighter 7.a4, 7.Nf3 and 7.h4 are covered only from Black's side in the sources (Giri's chapters). They
are strategic: White locks the position and heads for a kingside pawn storm, while Black wants ...b6, ...Ba6 or
...Bd7-a4. They are left out in favour of the sharp main line.

The line to watch is the natural-looking recapture 11.cxd4?, which loses the exchange to ...Nxd4 and ...Qc3+: the
knight has to go to e2 first.

## The poisoned pawn: why Ne2 and f4
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Bb4 4. e5 c5 5. a3 Bxc3+ 6. bxc3 Ne7 7. Qg4 Qc7 8. Qxg7 Rg8 9. Qxh7 cxd4 10. Ne2 Nbc6 11. f4
arrows: Gf4f5 Gd1d3 Rc7c3 Rc6e5
squares: Yc3 Ye5 Rh7

White has taken two pawns and the h-pawn is passed; Black has the lead in development. The play is a race, and the tempo
count decides it.

- **Why 10.Ne2.** It guards c3, so the fork on c3 and a1 is gone, and it keeps the knight ready to recapture on
  d4. The price is the f1-bishop, which is blocked until f4, Qd3 and Ng3 or h4 free it.
- **Why 11.f4.** The e5 pawn is attacked by the queen and the c6-knight. f4 supports it, and the pawn can
  advance to f5 later.
- **The queen.** From h7 the queen returns to d3 to guard c3, with tempo on d4. Then Nxc3 regains a pawn and
  threatens Nb5-d6+.
- **The verdict.** LBB86 calls the main line level; BTF has White slightly better in its version; the ChessBase
  reviewer of Bologan's DVD calls the lines after 8.Qxg7 very sharp and notes that Black scored well in one
  sub-line. If that is too much to learn, the second weapon 8.Bd3 cxd4 9.Ne2 dxc3 10.Qxg7 Rg8 11.Qxh7 Qxe5 12.h4 leads
  by force to an ending that GM Giri thinks Black can hold, but only on a narrow path.

## The Steinitz: the e5-f4 chain and the f5 break
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. e5 Nfd7 5. f4 c5 6. Nf3 Nc6 7. Be3
arrows: Gf4f5 Gd1d2 Ge1c1 Gh2h4 Rc5d4 Rf7f6
squares: Yd4 Ye5 Rc8

After 4.e5 the f6-knight has to move, and 5.f4 builds the chain e5-f4 that gives White space on the kingside.
The structure is the same as in the Fantasy chapter.

- **White's plan.** Qd2 and O-O-O, then h4-h5, g4 or the rook lift Rh3-g3, and the break f4-f5 when the
  pieces are in place. The bishop on e3 holds d4 so that ...cxd4 can be answered by Nxd4. Karjakin's win
  against Carlsen (a blitz game in the 7...cxd4 line) is the model: h4-h5, Rh3 and a lasting space edge in an
  ending.
- **Black's plan.** The c-pawn hits d4, the knight on c6 adds to the pressure, and ...f6 attacks the base of the
  chain at e5. If White's pieces are slow, ...c4 and ...b5-b4 give a queenside attack. White has to keep the d4 point supported.
- **Why 5.f4 and not 5.Nf3.** Tomic calls 5.f4 White's essentially only good move: 5.Nf3 c5 loses a central
  pawn, and 5.Nce2 costs too much time. Harikrishna's course recommends 5.Nce2, so there is a disagreement
  the sources leave open.
- **When to take on c5.** In the sources White takes only in the ...Be7 line (8.Qd2 O-O 9.dxc5, where the bishop
  loses a tempo recapturing) and against 7...a6 8.a3 Qb6. Elsewhere the pawn on d4 stays and Nxd4 is the
  recapture.
- **Trap to avoid.** 9.O-O-O? c4! 10.f5 b5 gives Black a crushing queenside attack, according to the
  Black-side study; and 10.O-O-O? b6! after 9.dxc5 Qa5. The king goes to the queenside only after Black's
  queenside play has been slowed down.

## How the structure changes after 7.Be3
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. e5 Nfd7 5. f4 c5 6. Nf3 Nc6 7. Be3 a6 8. a3
arrows: Ga3b4 Gd1d2 Gh2h4 Rb7b5
squares: Yb4 Rb5

Tomic lists four main replies, and each wants something different from White:

- **7...cxd4 8.Nxd4 Bc5 9.Qd2 O-O 10.O-O-O** is the model line: White gets the rook on d4, long castling and the
  h-pawn. BTF finds it clearly better, Wikibooks slightly better. The queen-trade ending after 9...Bxd4 10.Bxd4 Nxd4
  11.Qxd4 Qb6 (Karjakin-Carlsen) also suits White.
- **7...a6** is the most played move in ChessBase's database. White plays 8.a3 (taking b4 away from the knight),
  then Nxd4 and long castling with h4. The wing storm is the race: White's pieces reach the kingside first.
- **7...Be7** has two plans for White: Be2 and short castling, or Qd2 and dxc5 with long castling (the tactical
  version). The BTF line 8...cxd4 9.Nxd4 Nxd4 10.Bxd4 Nb8 11.O-O-O is safer; 8...O-O 9.dxc5 Qa5 10.a3 is the
  critical test, and the evaluation of that line is disputed between Gajewski and the Black-side study.
- **7...Qb6** is met by 8.Na4 Qa5+ 9.c3 cxd4 10.b4: the queen is chased away and the f3-knight recaptures on d4.
- **7...Rb8** and **7...b6** are slightly dubious (Tomic). Against 7...Rb8 8.h4 is the simple answer, and
  8.a3 b5 9.Ra2 is a nice prophylactic idea that the ChessBase reviewer of Bologan's DVD singles out.

**Gaps.** 6...Qb6 and 6...Be7 (before ...Nc6) are covered by Bologan and Ponomariov but no moves for them were found. 4...Nfd7 is the
only fourth move treated in depth; 4...Ne4 is in file 5.

## The Rubinstein: space, a pin on d7 and no bad bishop
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 dxe4 4. Nxe4 Nd7 5. Nf3 Ngf6 6. Nxf6+ Nxf6 7. c3 c5 8. Be3 Qc7 9. Ne5 a6 10. Qa4+ Nd7 11. Bb5
arrows: Gb5d7 Ge5d7 Ga4d7 Gd4g7
squares: Rd7 Yd4

Black has solved the French bishop problem by capturing on e4. In return he has given White the centre and
a free development, and the plan is more important than the first moves.

- **7.Bd3** is the most played move. After 7...c5 White's Be3 (the Korobov line in Dreev and Basso's course)
  defends d4 and attacks c5. Naroditsky calls 7.Bd3 totally not dangerous and shows 8.dxc5 Bxc5 9.O-O O-O
  10.Bg5 b6 as completely equal.
- **7.c3** (the Kramnik line, Naroditsky's choice) builds the chain d4-c3. The pin on d7 is the idea: Be3, Ne5,
  Qa4+ and Bb5 all attack the knight on d7. Playing 8.Ne5 at once leads to a queen-trade ending that
  Naroditsky finds unappealing; with 8.Be3 and 9.Ne5 White keeps the queens on.
- **Pawn breaks and castling.** Black wants ...c5 at once. White meets it with Be3 (or with the c3-d4 chain),
  and the sources castle long when the lines allow it (7.Bd3 b6 8.Qe2 Bb7 9.Ne5 Be7 10.Be3 O-O 11.O-O-O, and
  13.O-O-O in the 7.c3 line). Ne5 is the key square in both lines, and ...Bd6 challenges it.
- **The honest bit.** The 7.c3 line is a draft, and 7.Bd3 has no sharp sourced continuation after
  8...Qc7 9.O-O Bd6 10.Qe2 O-O.

## Fort Knox, 4...Nf6 and what is missing
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 dxe4 4. Nxe4 Bd7 5. Nf3 Bc6 6. Bd3 Nd7 7. O-O Ngf6
arrows: Gf3e5 Gc2c3 Rc6e4
squares: Yc6

- **Fort Knox (4...Bd7).** Black trades his bad bishop for a knight and gets a solid but passive Caro-Kann-like
  structure. The White-side sources are thin: BLPgN1cw (a Black-side study) gives 9.Be2 Bd6 and the 9.c3 exchange
  line as playable for Black; Craze has the space-grabbing 5.c4!?; the outlines of Bologan and Ponomariov
  both give the Fort Knox a chapter, but no moves from them were found. It is a gap.
- **4...Nf6 5.Nxf6+.** After 5...Qxf6 6.Nf3 White gains a tempo on h6 or Nc6 (PfVEpqnZ), and after 5...gxf6
  (the Rozman French) BTF has 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Bc4. These are given as variations.
- **4...Be7?!** is met by 5.Nf3 Nf6 6.Nxf6+ Bxf6 7.c3: the bishop on f6 stares at the chain.

## Rare third moves and 4...Ne4
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 c5 4. exd5 exd5 5. Bb5+ Nc6 6. dxc5
arrows: Gb5c6 Rd5d4
squares: Rd5

- **3...c5 (the Marshall Gambit).** Wikibooks marks it dubious. 4.exd5 exd5 5.Bb5+ and dxc5 keeps the pawn
  (Craze); the quick 5.dxc5 Bxc5 6.Qxd5 is also good, and 4...Qxd5? loses the queen to the knight on c3.
- **3...Nc6 (Hecht-Reefschlager).** Black blocks his own c-pawn. Wikibooks has 4.Nf3 Nf6 as equal; Craze meets 5.e5 Ne4 with
  6.Bd3 Bb4 7.Bd2.
- **4.e5 Ne4 (against the Classical).** 5.Nxe4 dxe4 6.Bc4 and a quick Ne2, c3 and O-O make the pawn on e4 a target;
  6...c5 7.d5! is the sharp punishment.
- **Not covered.** 3...Be7, 3...a6 and 3...b6: the only move text found is in one-like studies, so they are left
  out rather than guessed. 4...Qd7 in the Winawer is also missing.

## Link to the Fantasy chapter
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 e6 4. Nc3 Nf6 5. e5 Nfd7 6. f4 c5 7. Nf3 Nc6 8. Be3
arrows: Gf4f5 Gd1d2 Rc5d4
squares: Yd4 Ye5

The Caro-Kann Fantasy chapter (4.Nc3 Nf6 5.e5 Nfd7 6.f4 c5 7.Nf3 Nc6 8.Be3) arrives at exactly the position of
this chapter's Steinitz after 7.Be3. The pawns on d4, e5 and f4 and the pieces on c3, e3 and f3 are identical, and
so are Black's. So the plans of this chapter (Qd2, O-O-O, h4-h5, Rh3, f5) are the same ones the Fantasy player
uses, and learning the Steinitz here covers the Fantasy's main line as well.
