The Fantasy is the Caro-Kann answer for a player who likes what the Vienna Game with f4 gives: a pawn centre,
a half-open f-file and a bishop on c4 aiming at f7. After 3.f3 dxe4 4.fxe4 White owns e4 and d4 for a single
tempo and every piece development points at f7. Compared with the Exchange (the symmetrical position with
nothing to play for) this is a real fight in which Black has to know several things at once.

It is a weapon and not an advantage. In the Lichess elite database (2400+, about 7,200 games after 3.f3)
White scores about 54%, an ordinary first-move result, and in the main lines Black can equalise. The
pay-off is practical: the lines are rich in traps, most of which punish natural moves (6...Nf6?, 8...Nxe4?
and 8...O-O? in the gambit line), and the positions are easy for White to understand. The risks are real too:
f3 takes the knight's square and the pawn that shields the king, so the e1-h4 and a7-g1 diagonals are the
targets (...Qh4+, ...Bc5, ...Qb6 and ...Bxg1 ideas), and in the Qb6, g6 and e6 lines White gets no more than
equality.

The repertoire in this chapter: against 3...dxe4 play 4.fxe4 e5 5.Nf3, then 6.Bc4 against 5...Bg4, the gambit
6.Bc4 against 5...exd4 and 6.c3 against 5...Be6; against 3...e6 play 4.Nc3; against 3...Qb6 play 4.Nc3 dxe4
5.Bc4; against 3...g6 play 4.Nc3 Bg7 5.Be3 and meet ...Qb6 with b3. Rare tries are met by 4.Nc3 or 4.dxe5. The
Advance (3.e5) has its own chapter.

## What 3.f3 is for, and what it costs
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3
arrows: Gf3e4 Gf1c4 Rd8h4 Rf8c5
squares: Rg1 Re1

The pawn guards e4, so after ...dxe4 the f-pawn recaptures: White gets e4 and d4 without moving a piece,
and the f-file points at f7. If Black does not take, e5 followed by f4 gives a French-type structure and
the f3 pawn gets a purpose.

- **Gains:** the centre, the half-open f-file, quick development with Bc4 and castling.
- **Costs:** the g1 knight cannot use f3, so it goes to f3 only after the pawn has left (or to e2 or h3);
  the diagonals e1-h4 and a7-g1 have no pawn on them, so ...Qh4+, ...Bc5 and ...Bxg1 are always in the air;
  castling short can run into ...Bc5 and ...Qb6 pressure on g1, which is why the king so often steps
  to h1.
- **What kind of game:** open and tactical after 3...dxe4, a French or a Steinitz after 3...e6, a guarded
  fight for the dark squares after 3...g6 and 3...Qb6.

## The position after 5.Nf3
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. Nf3
arrows: Gf3e5 Gf3d4 Re5d4 Rc8g4
squares: Ye4

After 3...dxe4 4.fxe4 Black has one move to fight the centre, 4...e5, and then White develops with 5.Nf3. The
three replies to know are 5...Bg4 (about half of the games), 5...Be6 (29%) and 5...exd4 (16%). 5.dxe5? loses
the plot: after 5...Qxd1+ or 5...Qh4+ the pawn is a liability, the king is stuck and White has nothing for it.

- **5...Bg4** pins the knight so that ...exd4 wins a pawn: answer 6.Bc4 and castle.
- **5...exd4** takes the pawn: answer 6.Bc4 and play the gambit (Ng5 and Nxf7 tricks).
- **5...Be6** keeps the pawn centre and stops Bc4: answer 6.c3 and Bd3.
- **5...Nd7 and 5...Nf6** are rare and transpose or lose time.

## 5...Bg4 6.Bc4: the pin and the fork on f7
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. Nf3 Bg4 6. Bc4 Nd7
arrows: Gc4f7 Gf3e5 Rg4f3 Rd7e5
squares: Rf7 Ye5

The knight on f3 is pinned, so Black seems to threaten ...exd4. White does not defend: 6.Bc4 attacks f7, and
the pin is not a real pin because Bxf7+ and Nxe5+ fork the king and the bishop. That is why Black has only
one useful sixth move.

- **6...Nd7** defends e5 and prepares ...Ngf6 and ...Bd6. This is the only move that keeps everything
  together.
- **6...Nf6?** loses a pawn to 7.Bxf7+ Kxf7 8.Nxe5+ (a fork) 8...Kg8 9.Nxg4 Nxg4 10.Qxg4: in the elite
  database White won 28 of 35.
- **6...exd4** gives White a free development after 7.Qxd4 Qxd4 8.Nxd4, with Black's bishop loose and f7 soft.
- **Why castle and play c3 and not defend d4 directly:** the pin makes Black's ...exd4 look strong, but once
  the king is on g1 and c3 is played the knight is no longer needed on the d1-h5 diagonal, and ...exd4 is
  simply met by cxd4.

## The main-line set-up: c3, Bg5, Nbd2
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. Nf3 Bg4 6. Bc4 Nd7 7. O-O Ngf6 8. c3 Bd6 9. Bg5 O-O 10. Nbd2
arrows: Gg5f6 Gd1e1 Ge1h4 Gc4b3
squares: Re4 Yd4

After 7.O-O Ngf6 8.c3 the d4 pawn is fully supported, ...exd4 stops being a threat, and the pawn on e4 is bait:
8...Nxe4? 9.Bxf7+ Kxf7 10.Ng5+ is a double check (knight and f1 rook) and the g4 bishop falls. Black plays
8...Bd6 and White develops with 9.Bg5 (pinning the f6 knight) and 10.Nbd2.

- **White's plans:** Qe1 and Qh4 to hit the pinned f6 knight and the g4 bishop; Bb3 so that ...b5 loses its
  point; the break e4-e5 when it hits both Bd6 and Nf6; Rae1 and Nh4 towards the f-file.
- **Black's plans:** ...Qc7 and ...Rae8 against e4 and d4; ...b5 to drive the bishop away; ...h6 followed by
  ...Bh5-g6 to hit e4; ...exd4 once the pin on f3 has done its job.
- **Verdict:** equal. The studies stop at move ten and call the position attractive for White because Black
  has no clear plan. The elite results after 10...Qc7 11.Qe1 are not kind to White, so treat this as a line to
  understand and to win on traps, not as a forced edge.

## 5...exd4 and the pinned knight on f6
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. Nf3 exd4 6. Bc4 Nf6 7. O-O Bc5 8. Ng5 O-O 9. Nxf7 Rxf7 10. Bxf7+ Kxf7
arrows: Gf1f6 Gd1h5 Gh5c5
squares: Rf6 Rf7 Yc5

This is the line with the best results in the chapter (65% for White in elite games). The idea: do not
recapture on d4, develop with tempo against f7 (Bc4, O-O, Ng5), and let Black's king pay for the extra pawn.
After 6...Nf6 7.O-O Bc5 8.Ng5 Black castles into the combination: 9.Nxf7 Rxf7 10.Bxf7+ Kxf7 leaves his king
on the f-file with the f6 knight pinned in front of the rook on f1.

- **With Bc5:** 11.Qh5+ Kg8 12.Qxc5 wins the bishop (the pinned knight cannot take the queen).
- **With Be7 or no bishop on c5:** 11.e5 wins the pinned knight; the elite score of 11.e5 is 20 wins in 31
  games.
- **The gambit's price:** if Black avoids the trap (7...Be6 8.Bxe6 fxe6 9.e5), White regains d4 with a small
  initiative. The line works because Black's natural moves (castling and developing) fall into it.
- **Alternatives for Black:** 6...Bg4 7.Bxf7+ and 6...Bb4+ 7.c3 dxc3 8.Bxf7+ lead to sharp but unclear play;
  7...Nbd7 and 6...Nd7 are playable.

## 5...Be6: the quiet way and the king on h1
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. Nf3 Be6 6. c3 Nd7 7. Bd3 Ngf6 8. O-O Bd6 9. Kh1
arrows: Gc1g5 Gf3g5 Rd6h2
squares: Yg1 Re6

Black keeps the e5 pawn and the bishop covers c4, so Bc4 is pointless. White plays 6.c3 (supports d4, allows
Qb3 against b7 and e6) and 7.Bd3 (supports e4, eyes h7), castles and takes the king off the a7-g1 diagonal with
9.Kh1. This is a manoeuvring game; the elite database gives White 63% with 9.Kh1.

- **White's plans:** Bg5 or Ng5, Qe1-h4, Rae1 and a later dxe5 to open the f-file; a4 to fix the queenside.
- **Black's plans:** ...exd4 cxd4 to free the pieces and put a knight on b4; ...Qc7 and ...Rae8; ...Bg4 to
  bring the last piece out.
- **6.Bg5** (the other study line) leads to a level position by trades and is mainly a pattern.

## 3...e6: the Fantasy as a French
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 e6 4. Nc3 Nf6 5. e5 Nfd7 6. f4 c5 7. Nf3 Nc6 8. Be3
arrows: Ge3d4 Gf3d4 Rc5d4 Rf7f6
squares: Ye5 Rd4

Black declines the pawn and builds a French. Then f3 is a plain extra move, so White's best plan is to use it:
after 4.Nc3 Nf6 5.e5 the pawn goes to f4 and the position is the Steinitz Variation of the French, with an
extra move for each side. The study says Black is "down in development", but the count shows both sides have
used one extra pawn move, so the position is an ordinary Steinitz French: equal with correct play, and
rich in attacking chances for White if you know the French.

- **4...Bb4** pins the knight: 5.Bf4 (the study's choice) Ne7 6.Qd3 b6 7.Nge2 and White has the centre; 5.Bd2
  and 5.a3 are more common and score about the same.
- **4.Be3** is the alternative fourth move: 4...Nf6 5.e5 Nfd7 6.f4 transposes; 4...dxe4 5.fxe4 Qh4+ 6.g3
  Qxe4 7.Qe2 is a pawn sacrifice with good elite results but no explanation I found.
- **The French plans:** White keeps the chain e5-f4, supports d4 and aims at the kingside; Black hits d4 with
  ...cxd4 and ...Qb6, and undermines e5 with ...f6.

## Black's anti-Fantasy systems: 3...Qb6, 3...g6, 3...e5
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 Qb6 4. Nc3 dxe4 5. Bc4
arrows: Rb6b2 Rb6d4 Gc4f7 Rb6f2
squares: Rb2

These lines work because they use the weaknesses that f3 creates, and they are why the Fantasy is not an
advantage for White.

- **3...Qb6** hits b2 and d4. 4.Nc3 dxe4 and now 5.Bc4: if 5...exf3 6.Nxf3 is a gambit for development; if 5...Nf6
  6.fxe4 e5 7.dxe5 Ng4 8.Qe2 Nxe5 9.Bb3 is level. Do not play 5.fxe4 e5 6.dxe5?: after 6...Be6 Black's pieces
  develop with tempo and the extra pawn falls. The quiet 4.a4 a5 5.exd5 cxd5 6.Nc3 Nf6 7.Nb5 is a good practical
  try.
- **3...g6** 4.Nc3 Bg7 5.Be3: the bishop fianchetto aims at the dark squares around the king. 5...Qb6 6.b3
  (not the study's 6.Qd2 Qxb2) keeps the pawn; 5...dxe4 6.fxe4 Nf6 7.Nf3 Ng4 8.Bg1 is awkward but playable.
- **3...e5** 4.dxe5 Bc5 5.Nc3 Qb6 6.Na4 Qa5+ 7.c3 Bxg1 8.Rxg1 dxe4: White's king has lost castling rights
  but Black's pressure is spent. Level.

## Safety rules and typical mistakes
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. f3 dxe4 4. fxe4 e5 5. dxe5 Qh4+ 6. g3 Qxe4+
arrows: Rh4e1 Rh4e4 Rd8h4
squares: Re1 Rg1

The same few mistakes recur, and they are the reason this is a weapon you can win with.

- **Never take on e5 early.** 5.dxe5? gives Black the d-file (Qxd1+) or a check on h4 followed by ...Qxe4+; the
  extra pawn is not worth the king's safety.
- **Taking on e4 with the knight** (5.Nxe4 against 3...Qb6) is playable but makes the f3 pawn pointless: its
  whole job was to recapture there.
- **Watch the a7-g1 diagonal.** Whenever a black bishop reaches c5 or a queen reaches b6, ask what happens
  after ...Bxg1 or ...Qxb2. Kh1 solves most of these questions, which is why it is such a common move.
- **Bxf7+ only with a follow-up.** The sacrifice works when Nxe5+ forks (after ...Nf6?) or when the f-file
  pins a knight on f6 (after Nxf7). Otherwise it loses a bishop.
- **Do not hurry e4-e5.** The thematic break needs Bd6 and Nf6 to be in the line of fire; against a prepared
  Black it only opens the long diagonal.
