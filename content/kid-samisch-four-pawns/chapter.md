These are White's two big-centre systems against the King's Indian. In the Saemisch (5.f3) White supports e4 with a pawn, builds Be3, Qd2 and long castling, and often follows with an h-pawn storm. In the Four Pawns Attack (5.f4) he occupies the centre at once with all four pawns and tries to crush Black with e4-e5 before he can organise a counter.

The repertoire answers both the same way, with the break ...c5. Against the Saemisch it is 5...O-O 6.Be3 c5, which leads to three different structures (the Benoni after d5, an open centre after dxc5, a knight on e5 after Nge2 Nc6 d5) and has the support of nearly every popular study. Against the Four Pawns it is 5...O-O 6.Nf3 c5, which after 7.d5 e6 8.Be2 exd5 9.cxd5 produces a Benoni in which White's extra f-pawn is both strength and weakness: it gives him e4-e5, but it leaves e3 and g3 without pawn cover and takes the f-pawn's defence of the king away.

Second systems get their own games: in the Saemisch the classical 6...e5 with ...Nh5 and ...f5 (and the Panno 6...Nc6 as a rarer alternative), in the Four Pawns 6...e5. The notes try to say, for each move, what it takes away from the other side, because the structures repeat and the move orders are what matter.

## Why 6...c5, and how the Saemisch is built
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 c5
arrows: Gc7c5 Rc1h6 Rd1d2
squares: Yd4 Rh6

The move f3 gives White four things at once: e4 stays protected by a pawn for ever, the d1-h5 diagonal and d2 are free for the queen, the bishop on e3 can go to h6, and long castling with a pawn storm becomes possible. It costs him the f3 square for the knight (the g1-knight must go to e2, in front of the f1-bishop) and it leaves the pawn complex e4-f3-g2 to be attacked by ...f5, ...Nh5 or ...h5-h4.

Black cannot prevent the build-up, so he strikes at the centre before White has castled: ...c5 hits d4, keeps the g7-bishop open (after ...e5 it is blocked) and forces White to choose a structure. The three structures are:

- **7.Nge2 Nc6 8.d5 Ne5**: a knight on e5 and the h-pawn against the knight on g3 (first two games).
- **7.d5 e6**: the Benoni, with the Saemisch pawn on f3 (second file).
- **7.dxc5 dxc5 8.Qxd8 Rxd8 9.Bxc5**: Black gives up a pawn for development and a lead in the endgame (also second file).

## The Benoni structure after 7.d5 e6
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 c5 7. d5 e6 8. Qd2 exd5 9. cxd5 a6 10. a4 Re8
arrows: Gb7b5 Gb8d7 Gd7e5 Gh7h5 Re3h6
squares: Ye5 Rf3

After the exchange on d5 the structure is a Benoni, with the difference that White has played f3 and Black's dark bishop on g7 is not blocked. White wants to hold e4 with the pawn chain, develop the knight to g3 or c1 and castle; he often goes for the queenside, since the king on the kingside would meet ...h5-h4. Black's plan has three parts:

- **A knight on e5.** The b8-knight goes to d7 and e5, where it cannot be driven out by f4 (f4 would give up e4). Then ...Nfd7 or ...Nh5 brings the second knight to the party.
- **...h5-h4-h3** against a knight on g3. In the Saemisch Benoni the knight has few squares, and the pawn on h3 fixes g2 and keeps g4 weak.
- **...a6 and ...b5.** The b-pawn is a gambit pawn: with the rooks on b8 and e8 and the queen on a5, the opening of the b-file justifies the sacrifice. White's counter is a4, which stops ...b5 but makes b5 a pawn offer rather than a weakness.

The typical tactic is ...Bxg4 sacrificed against g2-g4: after fxg4 Nfxg4 the queen comes to h4 and White's king is stuck. The pawn on e4 can be taken with ...Nxe4 when the queen on d2 is overloaded.

## The knight on e5 and the h-pawn plan
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 c5 7. Nge2 Nc6 8. d5 Ne5 9. Ng3 h5 10. Be2 h4 11. Nf1 e6 12. Nd2 exd5 13. cxd5 h3
arrows: Gh5h4 Gh4h3 Ge5c4 Ge5f3 Rg2g1
squares: Ye5 Rf1 Rg2

This is the line the studies give most space to. After 8.d5 Ne5 White has to deal with ...Nxc4, so he plays 9.Ng3 (which also frees the f1-bishop to protect c4) or 9.Nc1. On g3 the knight is vulnerable to the h-pawn: ...h5 and ...h4 force it to f1, from where it needs two moves to return to the game. The pawn on h4 then goes to h3 as soon as the structure is fixed, so White has to play g3 and loses control of the dark squares f3, h3 and f2.

The knight on e5 is permanent: if f4 ever kicks it, ...Nfg4 or ...Neg4 hits Be3 and h2 and e4 is loose. That is why White's attempts to dislodge it with pawn moves usually end in a sacrifice, and why Black can afford the slow ...a6 and ...b5. The main defensive resource for White is to leave the king in the centre and use the queenside as a base: the endgame after queen trades is level.

## The pin on c3: 8.Qd2 Qa5
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 c5 7. Nge2 Nc6 8. Qd2 Qa5 9. O-O-O a6 10. Kb1 Rd8
arrows: Ra5c3 Ga5a2 Gd8d4 Gb7b5
squares: Rc3 Ya2

White's Qd2 puts the queen on a diagonal with the c3-knight, and 8...Qa5 uses it: the knight cannot move without allowing ...Qxd2+. The queen also looks at a2. White has two main replies:

- **9.O-O-O** commits the king. The attack is then g4 and h4, but Black's ...a6 and ...b5 arrive first and the king on b1 stands in front of the pawn Black has just broken. The calm answer is 10...Rd8, which prepares ...dxc5 with the rook hitting the queen. White can trade queens with Nd5, leaving a level endgame that Black can play for the win with ...f5.
- **9.d5 Ne5** and the knights go to b6 and e5: the knight on c3 is pinned, so c4 and a2 are under attack at the same time. White's c4-pawn needs two extra defenders, and the b5 break comes with tempo.

## The pawn sacrifice 7.dxc5
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 c5 7. dxc5 dxc5 8. Qxd8 Rxd8 9. Bxc5 Nc6 10. Nge2 b6 11. Ba3 a5
arrows: Gc8a6 Gc6b4 Ra3f8
squares: Yc4 Rc5

White takes the pawn, trades queens and then sits on it with the bishop. Black gets compensation because White's kingside is undeveloped (the Bf1 and the Ng1 are both locked behind f3 and e4), because the rook on d8 controls the open file, and because c4 will be weak once the bishop has to leave c5. Black's plan is always the same: a quick ...Nd7-e5 or ...Nb4, ...Be6 or ...Ba6 against c4, and ...Rac8; he regains the pawn in most lines.

The bishop on c5 has to leave once ...b6 or ...Nd7 attacks it, and from a3 it eyes e7 but does nothing for the king. White has several tenth moves (Nge2, Ba3, Rd1, Nd5, Be3), and the games take them one by one; against all of them the same recipe works.

## 6...e5: the closed set-up with ...Nh5 and ...f5
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 e5 7. d5 Nh5 8. Qd2 f5
arrows: Gh5f4 Gf7f5 Gf5f4 Gg6g5
squares: Yf4 Rg3

The classical way. The pawn on e5 shuts in the dark bishop, but it fixes the centre, and with f3 played White can neither play f4 nor keep the bishop on e3 without losing a tempo to ...f4. Black's pieces go to the kingside: ...Nh5 aims at f4 and g3, ...f5 breaks the e4-pawn, and ...f4 and ...g5 follow when White has castled long. It is a race, as in the Mar del Plata, and Black is better placed for it than there because White's own structure (f3, e4, Be3) is a target.

After 8.Qd2 the move 8...f5 is the main line. The Bronstein sacrifice 8...Qh4+ 9.g3 Nxg3 10.Qf2 Nxf1 11.Qxh4 Nxe3 gives up the queen for two bishops and pawns; White has scored well against it in the elite games, so it is shown only as a side-line.

## Bg5, Nge2 and the Panno
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f3 O-O 6. Be3 Nc6 7. Nge2 a6 8. Qd2 Rb8 9. Nc1 e5 10. d5 Nd4
arrows: Gb7b5 Gd4c2
squares: Yd4 Ya6

The side lines are met by moves that transpose into the main structures. After 6.Bg5 the bishop pins the f6-knight, but Black simply plays ...c5, 7.d5 e6 8.Qd2 h6, and the bishop has to choose between h4 and e3; it goes back to e3 and the game is the Saemisch Benoni with the extra moves Bg5-e3 and ...h6. After 6.Nge2 Black plays ...c5 as well, because 7.Be3 Nc6 is the main line, while 7.d5 e6 8.Ng3 exd5 9.cxd5 h5 is a Benoni in which the h-pawn gets its hit against the knight at once. The study's 6...a6 is an alternative: 7.Be3 Nc6 8.Qd2 Rb8 is the Panno.

The Panno 6...Nc6 is a different opening, with the plan ...a6, ...Rb8 and ...b5. In the main line White blocks the queenside with c5 or plays d5 at once, and Black's knight lands on d4. It is sharper than 6...c5 and less well mapped, and it is given as a single game.

## The Four Pawns: 6...c5 and the Benoni with f4
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f4 O-O 6. Nf3 c5 7. d5 e6 8. Be2 exd5 9. cxd5 Bg4
arrows: Gc7c5 Ge7e6 Gb7b5 Gg4f3 Rf4f5
squares: Ye4 Rd5 Re3

White's four pawns on c4, d4, e4 and f4 are the biggest centre in the opening, and they are held by pieces that cannot all be developed at once. Black attacks the centre where it is weakest. The pawn d4 is undermined with ...c5, and if White closes the centre with d5 the pawn on e4 becomes the base of the chain: it is then attacked by ...Nxe4, ...Re8 or ...Bg4, while the f4 pawn is the only pawn that supports e5. After 7.d5 e6 8.Be2 exd5 9.cxd5 Black has three plans:

- **9...Re8** pressures e4. White's answer is the break 10.e5; the lines are sharp.
- **9...Bg4** is the safe line: Black trades his bishop for the f3-knight and aims at ...Re8, ...a6 and ...b5 or ...c4.
- **9...Nbd7** is the quiet line from another study: the knight covers e5 so that the break is harmless, and Black plays ...Re8, ...a6, ...Qc7 and ...Rb8 or ...c4.
- **9...b5!?** is the gambit.

The pawn on f4 is also a weakness: with the f-pawn gone from f2, e3 and g3 are no longer covered by pawns, a bishop on e3 is hit by ...Ng4, and the king has lost the pawn that shelters it.

## White's e5 break and the b5 sacrifice
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. f4 O-O 6. Nf3 c5 7. d5 e6 8. Be2 exd5 9. cxd5 Re8 10. e5 dxe5 11. fxe5 Ng4 12. e6 fxe6 13. d6 Nc6
arrows: Gg4e5 Rd6e7 Gc8d7
squares: Rd6 Ye5

After 9...Re8 White's thematic break is 10.e5: if Black takes with 10...dxe5 11.fxe5 Ng4 the pawn on e5 is attacked three times (Ng4, Bg7, Re8) and White cannot hold it. 12.e6 fxe6 13.d6 is the usual way to keep the initiative: the pawn on d6 cramps Black's position, and the pieces come out with castling and the knight to e4. Black develops with ...Nc6 and ...Bd7, and the question is whether the attack on d6 and the weakness of e6 will be enough. White can also play 12.Bg5 Qb6 13.O-O, when 13...Nxe5 14.Nxe5 Bxe5 15.Bc4 Qxb2 has scored well for Black. In the main line 14...Bd7 is the study's move; the natural 14...Nd4 15.Ne4 has been very good for White. The trick 10.Qc2? Nxe4!! is in the second game, and 6...e5 as a second system is the last game.

The other tactical idea in this structure is against the bishop on d3. After 8.Bd3 exd5 9.cxd5, the move 9...b5! uses the fact that the bishop is loose and the pawn on e4 is attacked: if 10.Bxb5 then 10...Nxe4 11.Nxe4 Qa5+ forks the king and the bishop. White gets two pawns for the piece in the end, but his structure and king are broken. The same fork on the e1-a5 diagonal works wherever the e4 pawn has no support and the knight on c3 is gone.
