The Najdorf is the main line of the Sicilian and the most heavily analysed opening there is, but its logic is
compact. Black spends move five on ...a6 and then picks one of two structures: ...e5, which gives up the d5
square and leaves a backward pawn on d6, or ...e6, which keeps d5 and f5 covered but leaves Black cramped. The
King's Indian will feel half familiar, with its pawns on d6 and e5 and its dark-squared bishop that wants out,
but here the centre is open: the d-file is White's, the c-file is Black's, and d5 is a hole for a white knight
instead of a pawn that cramps Black.

This chapter covers White's two sharpest sixth moves. **6.Bg5** is met by 6...e6, and against 7.f4 the repertoire
is the delayed Poisoned Pawn, 7...h6 8.Bh4 Qb6: Black takes b2 and later a2 and e5 with the queen, plays a long
forcing sequence and ends up with three pawns for the exchange in a safe ending. 7...Be7 with Giri's 10...h6,
the Anand line 7...Nbd7, the immediate 7...Qb6, the Polugaevsky 7...b5 and 7...Qc7 get a game each, so that
the whole map is visible. Against the **English Attack, 6.Be3**, the repertoire is 6...e5 7.Nb3 Be7 8.f3 Be6 9.Qd2
and 9...h5, which stops the g4-g5 storm before it begins; 9...O-O with the full pawn race is the older and
sharper alternative, and 6...e6 and 6...Ng4 are also covered. The other sixth moves (6.Be2, 6.Bc4, 6.h3, 6.f4,
6.g3 and the rare ones) are in another chapter.

Everything here is taken from popular Lichess studies (the sources are listed at the end), cross-checked against
each other, and the explanations are written fresh. Several of the studies are written from White's side or give
the moves without comment; where that matters, the notes in the lines say so.

## What 5...a6 is for
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6
arrows: Rd4b5 Rc3b5 Rf1b5 Gb7b5
squares: Yb5

The pawn does three jobs. It takes b5 away from White's knights and bishop, and that is what makes ...e5
playable: without it, ...e5 is met by Bb5+ or Ndb5 and Nf5 settles on a square no black pawn can challenge. It
prepares ...b5, ...Bb7 and ...b4, the queenside expansion that supplies most of Black's counterplay. And it is a
waiting move: ...Nc6 or ...e6 would commit the pieces or the pawn structure before White has shown his hand,
whereas now Black chooses between ...e5 and ...e6 after seeing the sixth move. With the knight already on c6,
for example, 6.Bg5 and 6.Bc4 are harder to meet, because ...a6 and ...b5 would leave the c6 knight loose.

The price is a tempo spent on an edge pawn. Almost every Najdorf line is a race, and the extra pawn move is what
White's attacks are measured against: Black cannot afford slow moves, and has to know exactly which
counterattack is needed in each position.

## The ...e5 structure: the d5 hole and the backward pawn
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Be3 e5 7. Nb3 Be7 8. f3 Be6
arrows: Rc3d5 Rd1d6 Gc8e6 Gb8d7
squares: Yd5 Rd6

After ...e5 the d-pawn can only be defended by pieces, and it stands on a half-open file that White's queen and
rooks will use, especially after long castling. The d5 square can no longer be covered by a pawn, so White's
knight wants to settle there, and Black can only get rid of it by a trade. These two weaknesses are the price of
the structure; all of Black's piece play is built to make them harmless.

The assets are real. The light-squared bishop stands outside the pawn chain and is Black's good piece, so
...Be6 is the natural move: it covers d5 and c4, and offers a trade. The dark-squared bishop is the bad one: it goes
to e7, and its best prospect is a trade for White's bishop, with ...Ng4 or ...Bg5. The knight comes
to d7 and often on to b6, where it covers d5 and c4. The rook belongs on c8, on the file Black owns. And Black
has a central majority, d and e pawns against White's e-pawn, against White's extra c-pawn on the queenside:
...d5 is how Black cashes it in, and ...b5-b4 how he uses the smaller wing to attack.

## The ...e6 structure and why 6.Bg5 forces it
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Bg5 e6 7. f4
arrows: Rg5f6 Ge4e5 Gf4f5 Gd1f3
squares: Yd5 Yf5

6...e5? is a mistake after 6.Bg5: 7.Bxf6 Qxf6 8.Nd5 Qd8 9.Nf5 leaves White with knights on d5 and f5 that can never
be challenged. So Black plays 6...e6, which covers both squares, and Bxf6 can be answered with ...Qxf6. The price
is that the e-pawn no longer attacks the knight on d4, which stays powerful, and White gets a free hand: f4,
e4-e5, the queen to f3 or d2, long castling, and sacrifices on e6, d5 and f5.

Black's light-squared bishop is blocked by the e6 pawn and gets out through ...b5 and ...Bb7, which is why
a6 is such an important move here. The d6 pawn is a target for Qg3, and the king has to be handled carefully:
in many lines it stays in the centre for a long time. The usual Black counterplay is ...b5-b4 and ...Nbd7-c5,
and the break ...d5 is rarely available. The queen has a second job in the Poisoned Pawn lines, where she goes
to b6 and takes on b2.

## The ...b5-b4 expansion
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Be3 e5 7. Nb3 Be7 8. f3 Be6 9. Qd2 O-O 10. O-O-O Nbd7 11. g4 b5 12. g5 b4
arrows: Rb4c3 Rg5f6 Ga6a5 Ga5a4
squares: Rc3 Yb3

The pawn thrust ...b5-b4 hits the knight on c3, the piece that guards e4 and controls d5, b5 and a4. If the knight leaves, e4 is loose and Nd5 is no longer available as a threat; the pawn on b4
then supports ...a5-a4 against the knight on b3, and the a- and b-files open towards a king on c1 or b1.

Three things have to be checked before ...b5. First, the long diagonal: with the white queen on f3, a rook on
a8 can be hit by e5 or by Bxb5 combinations, so the bishop goes to b7 first or the rook to b8. Second, White's
sacrifices Bxb5 axb5 Ndxb5, which attack d6 and the queen on c7. Third, Nd5: when the knight arrives Black takes with the bishop
(...Bxd5 exd5 Nb6 hits the pawn), because ...Nxd5 would let exd5 attack the bishop on e6 with tempo. In the English Attack ...b5 is
usually delayed until White commits with g4; in the 6.Bg5 lines it is Black's reply to long castling.

## The ...d5 break
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Be3 e5 7. Nb3 Be7 8. f3 Be6 9. g4
arrows: Gd6d5 Ge6d5 Rg4g5
squares: Yd5 Ye4

If Black can play ...d5 safely he plays it: the pawn removes the backward pawn, opens the position for the
bishops and answers the pawn storm in the centre. The best-known example is 9.g4? d5!, when 10.g5 d4 11.gxf6 Bxf6
regains the piece with a better game, and 10.exd5 Nxd5 11.Nxd5 Bxd5 gives Black the stronger centre.

The condition is that White has not yet castled long with the queen on d2. Once the queen is on d2 and White's
rook is ready for d1, the same exchanges on d5 open the file for White, and Black is left with a weak king and
nothing to show for it, which is exactly why 9.Qd2 d5? is a mistake. So the break is a response, not a plan: it
works against an early g4 or an e4 pawn that has lost its support. It also saves the game in Giri's line
(19...d5!!) and appears with ...Nc5 and ...Ne4 against the h4 set-up in the 9.a3 line.

## Sacrifices for the white king: ...Bb3, ...a3 and ...Rxc3
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Be3 e5 7. Nb3 Be7 8. f3 Be6 9. Qd2 h5 10. Nd5 Bxd5 11. exd5 Nbd7 12. O-O-O Qc7 13. c4 a5 14. Kb1 O-O 15. Na1 a4 16. Nc2 a3
arrows: Ga3b2 Ga8a4 Gc7c4
squares: Rb1 Ya3

Wherever White's king sits on c1 or b1, its cover is the pawns a2, b2 and c2 plus a knight on c3 or b3. Black's
standard answer to a pawn storm is to remove that cover at any price. In the diagram the pawn on a3 forces
Nxa3 Rxa3 bxa3 Ra8, giving the exchange and a pawn for the open a- and b-files; objectively White is better,
but with the king on b1 it is hard to prove. In the old pawn-race line (9...O-O) the bishop goes
to b3 for the same reason: ...Bb3! axb3 axb3 opens the a-file at once. The exchange sacrifice on c3 is the third
version: when the white queen leaves the guard of c3, the threat of ...Rxc3 hangs over every queen move, and in
the 6.Bg5 lines White has to watch for it (in the Browne feint, for example, h4 and Rh3 are needed to meet it).

The rule is that the sacrifice must open a file at a king that has no minor piece in front of it, and that the
second wave, a queen on c7 or a5 and the other rook, is ready to come in. A pawn sacrifice does the same job
elsewhere: ...b4 in the pawn-race line, ...g5 against Qf3, and the Poisoned Pawn itself.

## The race: castling on opposite sides, and when Black keeps the king in the centre
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Bg5 e6 7. f4 h6 8. Bh4 Qb6 9. a3 Be7 10. Bf2 Qc7 11. Qf3 Nbd7 12. O-O-O b5 13. g4 Bb7
arrows: Rg4g5 Gb5b4 Gb7h1
squares: Ye8 Re4

Both players have a plan, and the first one to break through wins: White advances g and h, Black advances b
and a. The trade-offs are concrete: every move spent on defence is a tempo lost on attack, and a king that has
castled short walks into the pawns that are coming. That is why Black often leaves the king in the middle. In
the diagram it stays on e8: long castling runs into the open c-file, short castling invites g4-g5 and h4, and
the black pieces have enough to do (...g5! and ...Ne5 in the game) before the king needs to move.

The English Attack gives the same choice in a quieter form. After 9...O-O the king commits and the old pawn race
follows, with Black having to know the theory exactly. After 9...h5 the g4 break is gone, so the king can wait
in the centre until White decides where he castles: if White castles short with Be2 and Nd5, Black can castle
short too (13...O-O in the first game); if White castles long, Black plays ...b5 and ...Qc7 and keeps the king
in the middle.

## The delayed Poisoned Pawn
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Bg5 e6 7. f4 h6 8. Bh4 Qb6 9. Qd2 Qxb2 10. Rb1 Qa3 11. e5 dxe5 12. fxe5 Nfd7 13. Ne4 Qxa2 14. Rd1 Qd5 15. Qe3 Qxe5
arrows: Gf8c5 Ge8g8 Rh4d8
squares: Yd6 Re5

This is the position that the whole line is built for. Black has eaten b2, a2 and e5 with the queen, and has moved
nothing else: the pieces still at home are the problem, with Bd6, Nb5 and Qg3 looming. For the next three moves the game
is a race to catch up (...Bc5 pins the knight on d4, ...Qd5 heads for a5 and c5, ...O-O gets the king out), after
which Black trades queens and plays the ending with three pawns for the exchange.

Playing 7...h6 first is Black's way to make the line better. The bishop has to decide: Bxf6 gives up the bishop
pair for no threat (a game of its own), and on h4 the bishop is loose, so that ...Be7 can hit it. It also rules
out the quiet 9.Nb3?, which now loses the f4 pawn to ...Qe3+ because the bishop no longer guards it. The price is that White can
answer 8...Qb6 with 9.a3, protecting b2 and, after ...Be7, kicking the queen with Bf2; Black then goes to c7, and
the game returns to the ordinary Najdorf structure with a few extra moves. The line demands a lot of memory:
from move 11 almost every black move is the only move, and Caruana's 16.c3 with 18.Bc4!! is a serious test.
The reasons to choose it anyway are that both repertoire studies (Rob_Jobs and Chessboy197) pick it, that it
keeps the g4-g5 storms of the 7...Be7 main line away, and that its main line ends in a safe ending with
extra material.

## Tactics to know
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Bg5 e6 7. f4 Be7 8. Qf3 Nbd7 9. O-O-O Qc7 10. g4 h6 11. Bxf6 Bxf6 12. h4 Nb6 13. Be2 Be7 14. g5 Bd7 15. g6 fxg6 16. Qg4 O-O-O 17. Nxe6 Bxe6 18. Qxe6+ Kb8 19. Qxg6
arrows: Gd6d5 Rg6g7 Gb6d5
squares: Yd5 Rb2

The diagram combines most of the themes of the chapter: g5-g6, Nxe6 and the sacrifice of a piece against Black's
exposed king, answered by the quiet ...d5! that saves the game.

For Black:

- **...Qe3+** against Nb3 when the bishop has left g5 and f4 is loose, and the pawn grab on b2, a2 and e5 itself.
- **...Nxe4** after ...Be7 has attacked an undefended bishop on h4 (it works in the 9.a3 and 10.Nb3 lines).
- **...d5** in answer to g4, and the resource ...d5!! in Giri's line.
- **...Bb3** and **...a3** to open files, and **...Rxc3** when the knight on c3 is the only guard.
- **...g5** against Qf3 or the bishop on h4, giving a pawn for the e5 square.

For White, on every move:

- **Nd5**, to trade the f6 knight or settle on the hole; **Nf5**, when no pawn covers the square.
- **Nxe6** against a king that has not castled, and **Bxb5** when ...axb5 allows Ndxb5.
- **e5** with a queen on f3 or g3, hitting the rook on a8; **g5-g6** to break the pawns in front of the king.
- **Qh5** with a pin on f7 when the bishop on g5 hangs; **Bd6** to stop Black castling or to take c5 away from the queen.

Move orders matter. 7...h6 before ...Qb6 is the repertoire; 6.f3 e5 7.Nb3 Be6 8.Be3 Be7 is the English Attack by
transposition; in the English Attack 7...Be7 avoids 8.f4 and only then 8.f3 Be6 9.Qd2 h5 is played. Against the
quiet 7.Be2 and 7.Bd3 Black develops with ...Be7, ...O-O and ...b5; against the queen moves the queenside
pawns (...b5 and ...b4) are faster than White's attack.
