3.e5 is the answer to "equal and nothing to play for". The pawn takes f6 away from the knight, Black's light-squared bishop
stays shut behind its own pawns, and Black has to spend moves on ...c5 and ...Qb6 just to get counterplay. White keeps
every piece on the board, owns the space, and chooses between a slow squeeze (6.a3) and a gambit that goes straight at
the king (6.Bd3).

The repertoire. After 3...c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Black has three real fifth moves.

- **5...Qb6**: the main line. 6.a3 (b4, Bb2, the c4 clamp answered by Nbd2 and g3) is the main answer, and 6.Bd3, the
  **Milner-Barry Gambit**, is the sharp second weapon with its own games. 6.Be2 is a playable quiet alternative.
- **5...Bd7** (the Euwe Variation): 6.Be2 as the main line, with the sharp alternative 6.Bd3, which transposes into the gambit.
- **5...Nge7, 5...Nh6, 5...cxd4**: 6.Na3, 6.Bd3 and the recapture 6.cxd4 respectively.

Whoever wants only sharp play can answer 5...Qb6 and 5...Bd7 with 6.Bd3 every time; the price is that the Euwe player has
the good reply 6...Rc8 and White must know 7.a3.

**Honest status.** No popular White-side Lichess study on this line turned up (the topic pages block scripts); the big
studies are written for Black. The moves come from the lines those studies give, from Wikibooks and Wikipedia, from the
published outlines of Marin's, Schneider's and Werle's courses, and from small studies on the gambit with 25 to 39 likes.
Where only a Black-side study or an outline exists, the line says so, and ends where the source ends. The last section before the sources lists the
gaps.

## The chain: who attacks which base
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3
arrows: Rc5d4 Rc6d4 Rf7f6 Gc3d4 Gf3d4
squares: Yd4 Ye5 Ye6

White's chain runs c3-d4-e5, with its head on e5 and its base on d4. Black's runs e6-d5, with its base on e6. The old rule
is to attack the base of the chain, and both players follow it: Black hits d4 with ...c5, ...Nc6, ...Qb6 and a knight on
f5, and hits the head with ...f6; White's lever is f4-f5 against e6 (or h4-h5 and g4 with the same idea).

The defence of d4 decides everything. If d4 falls to a knight recapture, e5 has no pawn behind it, and the sources return to this
point again and again (4.dxc5 and 4.Nf3 are treated sceptically by Wikibooks and the Black-side studies for that reason). So White recaptures on d4 with the c-pawn, and
after 4.c3 the pawn on d4 is defended by c3, Nf3 and the queen. Later it also gets Bb2 (after a3-b4) or a knight on c2 (after Na3).

## The light-squared bishop
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Qb6 5. Nf3 Bd7 6. a3 Bb5 7. b4 cxd4 8. Bxb5+ Qxb5 9. cxd4
arrows: Rd7b5 Gb4b5 Gb2b4
squares: Yb5

Black's c8-bishop is behind e6 and d5 and has one real route out: round the pawn chain to b5 or a4, usually to exchange it for
White's good bishop. The Wade Variation (4...Qb6 5.Nf3 Bd7), the Euwe Variation (5...Bd7), 3...Bd7 with ...a6 and 3...b6 with
...Ba6 are all versions of the same plan. Each costs time, which is why White can usually let it happen, but White should
choose the terms:

- let Black take on b5 and recapture with the queen, or answer ...Bb5 with b4 (gaining a tempo) or c4 (Wikibooks);
- never move the bishop to b5 yourself early: 6.Bb5? Nxe5 wins a pawn against 5...Bd7;
- after ...Bd7 the Milner-Barry bishop check is covered, so the gambit lines change and 6.Bd3 Rc8 7.a3 is the right
  precaution.

## b2, d4 and ...Qb6
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Qb6
arrows: Rb6b2 Rb6d4 Rc6d4 Gf1d3 Ga2a3
squares: Rb2 Yd4

The queen eyes two pawns. The bishop cannot come to e3 to guard d4 (5.Be3, the Krupeichik Variation, is rare: after ...Qb6 the b2-pawn has no defender, the Lichess study calls it an
inaccuracy, and after 4...Qb6 it simply loses the pawn), so the pawn on b2 is the price of every active move. White has three answers, and each is a different answer to that problem:

- **6.a3** solves it by pushing the pawn out of the queen's range: after b4 the c1-bishop is free, and Bb2 is the third
  defender of d4.
- **6.Bd3** ignores d4 and offers the pawn, because Bb5+ and the pin on the e-file are worth more than a pawn.
- **6.Be2** is the quiet developing move; the knight goes to c3-a4 with tempo against the queen.

6.Na3 (Na3-c2 to defend d4) is a fourth idea and is in the outlines of Marin and Jobava; the outlines give no more than the
first moves.

## 6.a3: the queenside expansion
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Qb6 6. a3 c4 7. Nbd2 Na5 8. g3 Bd7 9. h4
arrows: Gf1h3 Gh4h5 Gd2b3 Rc6a5 Ra5b3
squares: Yb3 Rc4

The pawn on a3 prevents ...Bb4 and ...Nb4 and prepares b2-b4. Black's reply 6...c4 stops b4 for good, because b2-b4 would be
taken en passant, and it locks the f1-bishop behind the c4-pawn. In return Black gives up the pressure on d4. The fight is
then about **b3**: Black wants a knight on a5 and a bishop on d7-a4 to land there; White's knight on d2 covers it.

- **8.g3** and h4 is the sharp setup (Marin gives it two chapters): the bishop goes to h3 or g2, the h-pawn advances, and
  White plays on the kingside where Black's pieces are not. 8.Be2 (Rb1, b3) is the quiet alternative.
- **6...Nh6** (the Lputian Variation) is met by 7.b4: ...cxd4 8.cxd4 Nf5 9.Bb2 gives White three defenders against Black's
  three attackers on d4. Do not take on h6.
- **6...cxd4** is met by 7.cxd4 and then b4 and Bb2; **6...Bd7** by 7.b4; **6...a5** by the gambit
  7.Bd3, because the pawn on a5 has left b5 undefended.

## 6.Bd3: the gambit and the pin on e6
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Qb6 6. Bd3 cxd4 7. cxd4 Bd7 8. O-O Nxd4 9. Nxd4 Qxd4 10. Nc3 Qxe5 11. Re1
arrows: Ge1e6 Gc3d5 Gc3b5 Gd3b5
squares: Re6 Yd5

The idea is to let Black take d4 and e5 and pay with tempi. The e-file opens, the queen is dragged
to the centre, and the knight lands on d5 or b5. The pin on the e6-pawn is the central motif: once the queen has left the e-file
the pawn on e6 cannot recapture on d5, so Nxd5 works, and Nb5-c7+, Bf4-c7 and Rc1 come with it.

- **The first trap**: 7...Nxd4? 8.Nxd4 Qxd4?? 9.Bb5+ wins the queen. That is why Black must play 7...Bd7.
- After 8.O-O the knight pair is traded on d4. 9.Nbd2 (keeping Nf3) and 9.Ng5 (a quick attack on f7) are the alternatives.
- Black's best defence is 11...Qb8, giving back the d5 pawn; the other queen moves lose quickly to tactics.
- The Hector form, 7.O-O, castles before recapturing and offers the second pawn on c3 instead.

## The ...f6 break and the knight on f5
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Bd7 6. Be2 f6 7. O-O fxe5 8. Nxe5 Nxe5 9. dxe5 Qc7 10. Re1 O-O-O
arrows: Rf6e5 Gf1e1 Gc1f4 Gf3h4
squares: Ye5 Rf6

...f6 is Black's other lever, against the head of the chain. It works when White spends a tempo on something else (6.a3?
against 5...Bd7 is the classic example, because without the queen on b6 the move hits nothing) and when Black is ready to take on e5
and play ...Qc7 and ...O-O-O. White's answer is to keep e5 supported (Bf4 rather than Be2, as Chess in the Library puts it)
or to take on e5 at a moment when the exchange opens the e-file for his rooks.

Black's second plan is the knight on f5, from e7 or from h6. It attacks d4 without blocking the f8-bishop (from h6) and it can be
chased, which is what the sources do: 9.g4 Nh4 10.Nxh4 Qxh4 11.Bf4, or Bd3 to take on f5 and give Black doubled pawns
after ...exf5. Against 5...Nh6, taking on h6 with the bishop is not the way, since the g-file is Black's compensation.

## Traps and when ...Qxb2 is poisoned
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Qb6 6. Bd3 cxd4 7. cxd4 Nxd4 8. Nxd4 Qxd4 9. Bb5+
arrows: Gb5e8 Gd1d4
squares: Rd4

- **Milner-Barry**: 7...Nxd4? 8.Nxd4 Qxd4?? 9.Bb5+ wins the queen (Wikipedia, Wikibooks, Levy). After 7...Bd7 8.O-O Nxd4 9.Nxd4
  Qxd4 10.Nc3 Qxe5 11.Re1 the study shows many club-level blunders: 11...Qd6 12.Nb5 Qb6 13.Be3 Bc5? 14.Bxc5 Qxc5 15.Rc1 wins the queen.
- **The dxc5 trap**: 6.Bd3 Bd7 7.dxc5 Bxc5 8.O-O Nge7? 9.b4 traps the bishop.
- **Sorensen**: 9.Ng5 h6 10.Nxf7 Kxf7 11.Qh5+ Ke7 12.Qh4+ g5 mates; Black has 12...Kf7 and the line is a practical weapon.
- **...Qxb2** is good for Black when White has just taken the bishop away from c1 himself: 6.a3 Nh6 7.Bxh6? Qxb2 wins two
  pawns. It is poisoned in the Hector gambit: 7.O-O dxc3 8.Nxc3 Bd7 9.Be3 Qxb2? 10.Nb5, and after 11.Re1 a6 12.Rb1 Qxa2 13.Re2
  Qa5 the move 14.Bb6!! traps the queen. That is why Black retreats with 9...Qd8.
- **Against 5...Bd7**: 6.Bb5? Nxe5 wins a pawn; 6.a3 f6 equalises.

## How this links to the Caro-Kann Advance
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2 c5 6. Be3 Qb6
arrows: Rb6b2 Rb6d4 Gd4c5
squares: Rb2 Yd4

The chain is the same one as in the Caro-Kann Advance chapter: d4-e5 against ...c5 and ...f6, with
the base on d4 and the knight on f5 as Black's favourite. The differences explain the choice of moves:

- In the Caro Black's bishop is outside the chain on f5 and the c-pawn needs two moves to reach c5, so White can play the Short
  System with Be3 (d4 is covered by the bishop and b2 is offered on purpose). In the French the bishop is inside and ...c5 costs
  one move, so 4.c3 is the main move and 5.Be3 only a rare try.
- The plan "gain queenside space with a3-b4" appears in both: in the Caro it is the answer to 3...c5 4.dxc5 e6 5.a3; here it is 6.a3
  against ...Qb6.
- The queen on b6 hitting b2 and d4 is the same pressure, and in both chapters one of the main answers is a pawn offer
  (the b2-pawn in the Short System, the d4 and e5 pawns in the Milner-Barry), while the other is a quiet queenside plan.
- The kingside plans (h4, g4 against a knight on f5, Bd3 to take on f5) are the same in both; so are the exchange ideas Nxf5 and Bxf5.
- c4 as the central break against an uncastled king appears in both: 5.c4! against 3...Bd7, 7.c4 in the Wade Variation.

## What is solid, and what is a draft
moves: 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. c3 Nc6 5. Nf3 Qb6 6. a3
arrows: Gb2b4 Gc1b2

- **Best sourced**: 6.a3 c4 7.Nbd2 Na5 8.g3 Bd7 9.h4 (Marin's outline and a 395-likes study), 6.a3 Nh6 7.b4 (Wikibooks,
  Schneider and three studies), the Milner-Barry main line to 11.Re1 (Wikipedia, Wikibooks, Marin's outline, the 395-likes study to
  10.Nc3, and three small studies with 25 to 39 likes for the deeper lines and the tactics).
- **Thin**: 5...Bd7 6.Be2 Nge7 7.O-O Ng6 8.g3 Be7 9.h4 (Schneider's outline only); 5...Nge7 6.Na3 (outline, ends at 8.Nc2);
  6.a3 Bd7 and 6...f6 (one source each); the Hector Gambit (two small studies and a ChessBase video that could not be viewed); every
  line from a Black-side study that stops at an equal position (the Euwe lines with 7...Nf5, the Wade and 4...Bd7 lines).
- **Missing**: 3...Nc6 (Wikibooks says White has an easy game, but gives no moves), 3...Ne7, 4...Nh6, 4...Qa5 and 4...Ne7
  (named in Schneider's outline, no moves found), 5...Qc7 (named in Wikibooks, no moves), 6.Na3 beyond its first moves, Werle's
  6.Be2 Bb5 line against 5...Bd7. These need a book or the courses themselves.
