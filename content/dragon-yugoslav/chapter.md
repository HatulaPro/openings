The Yugoslav Attack is the reason the Dragon has a reputation. White builds the King's Indian
Sämisch set-up against a fianchetto that has no ...e5 in it: Be3, f3, Qd2, queenside castling, then
h4-h5 to open the h-file and Bh6 to remove the bishop that defends the king. A King's Indian player
knows the bishop on g7 and the pawns in front of it; what is new is everything else. The centre is
half open, Black has no pawn on c7, and the game is a race in which the c-file plays the part that
the f-file and the pawn storm play in the King's Indian.

Black's assets are permanent and White's are not. Black has two centre pawns against one, a bishop
that points at b2 and c3, and a half-open file aimed at the white king. White has more space and the
faster-looking attack, but if the attack is held or the queens come off, Black is normally the one
who is comfortable. That is why so many lines here end with White forcing a draw or steering into a
slightly better ending, and why the opening rewards knowing exactly which defensive move is needed
and not merely the general plan.

The repertoire: against **9.Bc4** the Topalov Variation (10...Rc8 11.Bb3 Nxd4 12.Bxd4 b5), which is
Gawain Jones's main recommendation and the most played line among strong players today, with the
Soltis Variation behind it because White can force it by move order. Against **9.0-0-0** the break
9...d5, with 12...Bxd4 and 13...Qb6 in the main line. Against **9.g4**, 9...Be6. The other major
systems (the 12.Kb1 lines, the Chinese Dragon, 10...Qa5, Giri's 9...Nxd4, the old 12...e5, the
Dragadorf) each get a line of their own so that the whole map is visible.

## The tabiya and White's three roads
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6
arrows: Rf1c4 Re1c1 Rg2g4 Gd6d5
squares: Yd5

Everything White has played serves one plan. 7.f3 holds e4 and takes g4 from the knight, so that
the bishop on e3 cannot be chased; 8.Qd2 lines up Bh6 and clears the way for castling long.

- **9.Bc4** stops ...d5 by force and points the bishop at f7. The price is time: the bishop will be
  hit by ...Ne5 or ...Na5, and on b3 it is a target for ...a5-a4. This is the sharp road, covered in
  the first three groups of lines.
- **9.0-0-0** saves the bishop move and allows 9...d5. White gives up the direct attack for
  pressure in a half-open centre. Strong players choose it most often, because White needs to
  remember less and risks less.
- **9.g4** also discourages ...d5 (10.g5 would hit the knight that supports it) without spending a
  move on the bishop.

Move order matters less than it seems before move 9. Black can castle on move 7 or play 7...Nc6
first; the only real error is 6...Ng4, which loses material to 7.Bb5+.

## The c-file, the c4 square and the b-pawn
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Ne5
arrows: Ge5c4 Gc8c3 Gb7b5 Gd8a5
squares: Yc4 Yc3

Black's play runs down the c-file towards c3 and b2. The classical way to use it is ...Ne5-c4: the
knight attacks the queen and the bishop on e3, White has to take it with the b3-bishop, and after
...Rxc4 Black has removed the piece that both attacks f7 and covers the white king, and has a rook
on the fourth rank that can double, swing, or give itself up on c3. The queen joins from a5 and the
b-pawn runs to b4 to dislodge the knight that holds White's queenside together.

The Topalov Variation gets at the same targets in another way. Black trades knights on d4 first and
plays ...b5 at once: the bishop on b3 is then short of squares (...a5-a4 threatens to trap it), the
knight on c3 can be hit by ...b4, and White has no time for the slow h-pawn attack.

White's standard prophylaxis is **Kb1**. It takes the king off the c-file, covers a2, removes
checks on the c1-h6 diagonal and makes Nd5 possible in positions where ...Qxd2 would otherwise
come with check. The quiet 12.Kb1 is considered the critical test of 11...Ne5 for exactly this
reason: Black's standard moves all become a little less effective.

## The exchange sacrifice on c3
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Ne5 12. h4 Nc4 13. Bxc4 Rxc4 14. h5 Nxh5 15. g4 Nf6 16. Bh6 Nxe4 17. Qe3 Rxc3
arrows: Gg7c3 Gd8a5
squares: Rc3 Rb2 Ra2

The move to learn first. A rook for the knight on c3 wrecks the pawns in front of the white king,
removes the only defender of e4, a2 and d5, and leaves the Dragon bishop without an opponent on
the long diagonal. Black rarely needs to win material back quickly: a pawn and the shattered
structure are normally enough even after a queen trade, and with queens on the white king is in
danger for the rest of the game.

- It is strongest when the white queen has been drawn away, typically after Bh6 Bxh6 Qxh6: the
  queen on h6 attacks but no longer defends c3 or b2.
- It works as defence as well as attack. Without the knight on c3 there is no Nd5, and White's
  attack usually needs that move to remove the knight on f6.
- It should be checked, not played by reflex, when White has already played Kb1 and can answer
  with bxc3 and c4, or when Black has no second wave (queen to a5 or c7, the other rook to c8).

The diagram is a classical example: Black has taken on e4 with the knight because the queen on d2
was tied to the bishop on h6, and now gives the rook to keep the extra pawns and the bishop.

## The race on the h-file and why Black plays ...h5
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Ne5 12. h4 h5
arrows: Rg2g4 Re3h6 Re3g5 Gc8c5
squares: Yh5 Rg5

White's attack has three parts: open the h-file with h4-h5 (as a pawn sacrifice if need be),
exchange the g7-bishop with Bh6, and remove the knight on f6 with Nd5, g4-g5 or Bxf6. When all
three succeed, the queen and rook mate on h7 or h8. Black cannot stop all of them by passive
means, which is why the old main lines with ...Nc4 and the h-file open were a pure race.

The Soltis move 12...h5 changes the terms. The h-file stays closed unless White gives up material
with g4, and after g4 hxg4 Black has an extra pawn and a g-file that is blocked by his own pawn.
The cost is the g5 square: the bishop goes there, pins the knight against e7 and prepares e5 or
Bxf6. Black's answer is ...Rc5, a rook that defends along the fifth rank and is ready to take the
bishop on g5: that sacrifice drags the h4- or f4-pawn off its file, kills f4-f5 and gives the
knight a permanent post on e5.

The same logic explains the modern habit of **delaying castling** (the Dragadorf and the early
...h5 lines). A king that has not gone to g8 gives h4-h5 nothing to open, and ...h5 played before
White has committed anything stops g4 and Bh6 for good. What Black gives up is time and the
...d5 break.

## What the Dragon bishop is worth
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. O-O-O d5 10. exd5 Nxd5 11. Nxc6 bxc6 12. Bd4 e5 13. Bc5 Be6
arrows: Rc5f8 Gg7h6
squares: Yg7

More than a rook, in many positions. The bishop defends the king and attacks b2 with the same
move, and the dark squares around both kings belong to whoever keeps a dark-squared bishop. The
theory is full of lines where Black lets a rook go to keep it: in the diagram Black has left the
rook on f8 to be taken, because after Bxf8 Qxf8 the bishop pair and the dark squares give more
than enough. The rook lift to c5 followed by ...Rxg5 is the same trade.

The practical rules:

- When White plays Bh6, the usual answer is ...Bxh6, pulling the queen to h6, followed by ...Rxc3
  or a queen move with tempo. With a rook already on e8 Black can keep the bishop with ...Bh8,
  which is the main point of the waiting move ...Re8.
- When White plays Bd4 to offer the trade, Black decides by the pawn structure: in the Topalov line
  it happens on g7 and Black is fine because the queenside play is already rolling; after 9.0-0-0 d5
  the choice between 12...Bxd4 and 12...e5 is the choice between a sound structure and keeping the
  bishop.
- White's Nd5, when it forces ...Nxd5 and Bxg7, removes both of Black's kingside defenders at once.
  Check what the recapture on d5 does before allowing it.

## The Topalov plan
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Nxd4 12. Bxd4 b5
arrows: Ga7a5 Ga5a4 Gb5b4 Rc3d5
squares: Rb3 Ya4

Trading on d4 looks like a concession, since it brings the bishop to the long diagonal opposite
ours. The point is tempo. With the knight gone from d4, ...b5 cannot be met by Ndxb5, and the threat
of ...a5-a4 makes White react on the queenside before the kingside attack has started. If White
plays a3 to give the bishop a square on a2, then ...b4 opens a file whatever White does, usually as
a pawn sacrifice.

White's best answer is 13.Nd5, exchanging both the f6-knight and the bishops. That leads to the
structure of the next section, and to a main line that has been analysed out to a forced draw. Every
other 13th move gives Black the initiative: the lines are short and worth knowing exactly, because
several of them (14.a4, 13.Bxa7) lose quickly for White.

Why this and not 11...Ne5: after 11...Ne5 White has 12.Kb1, the line the theoreticians trust most
for White, and Black has to choose between several difficult defences. The Topalov move order
cuts it out. What it cannot cut out is the Soltis, because White can start with 10.h4 and after
10...h5 the knight belongs on e5 again. So both systems have to be known.

## The d5-pawn structure and the endings
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Nxd4 12. Bxd4 b5 13. Nd5 Nxd5 14. Bxg7 Kxg7 15. exd5 a5 16. a3
arrows: Rh1e1 Re1e7 Gb5b4 Gc8c5
squares: Re7 Yc5

When White answers ...Nxd5 with exd5 the game changes character. The e-file is half open and e7 is
a fixed target; White doubles on it and Black cannot play ...e6 without leaving d6 weak. In return
the bishop on b3 bites on its own pawn, the c-file is Black's, and c5 is a fine square for a rook
that defends e7 sideways from e5 or c7 when needed. Black's counterplay is ...b4, and it is
usually worth a pawn: the a- and b-files open against a king that has no minor piece near it.

Endings in the Dragon are better for Black than the opening's reputation suggests.

- With the usual structure (e7 and d6 against c2, and the white f- and g-pawns advanced), Black's
  extra centre pawn and the weaknesses White made while attacking count for more as pieces come
  off. Giri's repertoire heads for such endings on purpose.
- After an exchange sacrifice on c3, rook and knight or rook and bishop against two rooks is often
  easy to play for Black as long as one pawn has been collected: White's queenside pawns are
  targets and the rooks have no open file.
- After 9.0-0-0 d5 and ...bxc6 it is the other way round. Black has the split queenside pawns and
  White the c5 outpost, so there Black needs the pieces on and the b-file active; drifting into a
  quiet ending is how those lines are lost.
- In the pawn-grab line 12.Nxd5 cxd5 13.Qxd5 Qc7 White can take both rooks for the queen. Material
  favours White, coordination favours Black, and practice says the queen is at least as good.

## The ...d5 break
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. O-O-O d5 10. exd5 Nxd5 11. Nxc6 bxc6
arrows: Ga8b8 Gd8b6 Re3d4 Rc3d5
squares: Yc5 Yb2

If Black can play ...d5 safely, Black plays it: the break frees the position and removes the only
structural problem, the hole on d5. After 9.0-0-0 it is a pawn sacrifice. White can win the pawn
with 12.Nxd5 cxd5 13.Qxd5, but then 13...Qc7 opens three files and two diagonals against the white
king, and the theory has held for Black for decades.

So White normally declines with 12.Bd4, asking to trade the bishop. The position that results is
the main line of the whole Dragon today: Black has two pawn islands on the queenside and a hole on
c5, White has a king that sits on the open b-file's target square. Black's plan is concrete: queen
to b6 with tempo, then to c7, rooks to d8 and b8, the bishop to f5 or e6, the knight to f4 or b6,
and ...h5 to hold up g4. White tries to plant a knight on c5 and trade pieces.

The tenth-move alternatives (10.Qe1, 10.Kb1, 10.Nxc6) all try to get the benefits of the main line
without allowing this easy development; each has one precise answer, given in the lines.

## Tactics to know
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 O-O 8. Qd2 Nc6 9. Bc4 Bd7 10. O-O-O Rc8 11. Bb3 Nxd4 12. Bxd4 b5 13. h4 a5 14. a4 bxa4 15. Nxa4 Bxa4 16. Bxa4 Rc4 17. Bb3 Nxe4
arrows: Gc4d4 Gg7d4
squares: Rd4 Rd2

For Black:

- **...Rxc3**, above. Its cousins are ...Rxc4 structures where the rook is sacrificed on c4 after
  b3, and ...Rxb2 when the b-file is open.
- **...Nxe4**. It works when fxe4 is impossible or bad because of what is uncovered: the bishop on
  g7 against d4 and b2, or a rook on c4 against d4, as in the diagram, where 18.fxe4 Rxd4 costs
  White at least a pawn and 18.Bxc4 Nxd2 the queen.
- **...Bxg4, ...Nxg4 and ...Nxf3**. Once White has played g4 and h5, a piece for two or three
  kingside pawns often ends the attack and leaves Black's pawns rolling.
- **...Bh6 or ...Qa5 with a pin on the c1-h6 diagonal**, when the king is still on c1.
- **...Nc3+ in-between moves** when the b-pawn has reached b4 and the knight is on d5.

For White, the ideas to watch on every move:

- **Bh6**, and after ...Bxh6 Qxh6 the threats Nd5, h5 and g4-g5.
- **h5** as a pawn sacrifice, and **g4** against the Soltis pawn, to open the h-file.
- **Nd5**, removing the f6-knight. With queens on d2 and a5 it carries the in-between check
  Nxe7+ after ...Qxd2, which is why Kb1 (so that ...Qxd2 is not check) changes so many lines.
- **Nf5**, a knight sacrifice on the square g6 guards, to open the g-file after ...gxf5.
- **e5**, opening the d-file against the bishop on d7 and clearing e4 for the knight.
- **Rxh5** in the Soltis, giving the exchange to reopen the file Black closed.

## Move orders, the Dragadorf and early ...h5
moves: 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6 6. Be3 Bg7 7. f3 a6 8. Qd2 h5
arrows: Gb7b5 Gb8d7 Gc8b7
squares: Yh5 Yg4

White has three ways to reach the 9.Bc4 lines and they are not equivalent.

- **10.0-0-0** allows the Topalov Variation (10...Rc8 11.Bb3 Nxd4), and after 11...Ne5 it keeps
  12.Kb1.
- **10.h4** must be answered by 10...h5. After 11.0-0-0 Rc8 12.Bb3 Ne5 the Soltis is on the board
  and White has avoided the Topalov, but has also spent h4, so the 12.Kb1 lines are gone.
- **10.Bb3** allows 10...Nxd4 11.Bxd4 b5 at once, the original form of Topalov's idea; 10...Rc8
  keeps both of the main systems available.

The Dragadorf (...a6 and ...b5 with the king in the centre, the knight on d7 and the bishop on b7)
and the lines with an early ...h5 are playable alternatives to the whole main-line complex. They
avoid most of the theory and the h-file attack; they give White a freer hand in the centre and
make ...d5 hard to arrange. One line shows how they are set up.
