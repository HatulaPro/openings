More than half of the 1.e4 g6 games in practice never see 3.Nc3: White plays 3.Nf3, 3.f4, 3.c3 or 3.Be3, or
leaves d4 for later with 2.Nf3, 2.f4, 2.Nc3 or 2.Bc4. Without a knight on c3, e4 has one defender fewer, and
that decides most of what follows. After ...d6 and ...Nf6 White has to show how e4 is held. Nc3 transposes
into the 3.Nc3 chapter. Bd3, Nbd2 and Qe2 keep the knight off c3 but give Black the ...d5 break that the Pirc
rarely gets. e5 needs a pawn on f4 behind it.

This chapter was built from the White moves that show up most often in real 3+2 games, wide rather than deep.
The sources for these sidelines are thinner than for the main lines. No book text could be read, and the
Lichess studies that cover them have 21 to 237 likes. Lines resting on one study, or on none, are marked as
drafts in their summaries and listed in the last section.

## Bd3 and c3: ...Nbd7, ...e5, then ...d5
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. Nf3 d6 4. Bd3 Nf6 5. O-O O-O 6. c3 Nbd7 7. Re1 e5 8. Nbd2 Re8 9. h3 b6 10. a4 a6 11. Nf1 Bb7 12. Ng3
arrows: Gd6d5 Ge5d4
squares: Ye4

The most common quiet set-up: Bd3, O-O, c3, Re1 and the knight going d2-f1-g3. With Bd3 and c3 in, d4 is
only held by c3 and Nf3, so ...Nbd7 and ...e5 hit it at once, and White cannot keep the tension for ever.
Black then finishes development with ...Re8, ...b6 and ...Bb7 and waits for the knight to leave d2. Once it
does, e4 has lost a defender and ...d5 is a real break, something the Pirc with a knight on c3 rarely allows.
The same position comes from 3.c3 d6 4.Nf3 Nf6 5.Bd3 and from the Pirc order 1.e4 d6 2.d4 Nf6 3.Bd3.

The elite players prefer 6...Nc6 to 6...Nbd7 here. The sources give ...Nbd7, which is what the lines follow.
After 6.Nbd2 (no source line), avoid 6...e5?!: it scores badly. Play ...Nc6 or ...c5 instead.

## The pin ...Bg4 when d4 is loose
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. Nf3 d6 4. Bd3 Nf6 5. O-O O-O 6. Re1 Bg4 7. h3 Bxf3 8. Qxf3 Nc6 9. c3 e5 10. d5 Ne7
arrows: Gf7f5 Gf6d7

...Bg4 works when White has castled or put the queen on e2 without c3, because the pin on f3 takes away d4's
only defender. Black gives the bishop for the knight, plays ...Nc6 and ...e5, and after d5 the knight goes
back to e7. The position is then a King's Indian where White's light-squared bishop sits behind its own e4
pawn, and ...Nd7 with ...f5 is the plan. Against c3 or h3 already played, the pin only loses time.

## 4.Bc4: the knight to c6 first
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. Nf3 d6 4. Bc4 Nc6
arrows: Ge7e5 Rc4f7

Against the bishop on c4 the sources put the knight on c6 before ...Nf6: it hits d4 and prepares ...e5,
and if White pushes d5 it goes back to e7 or b8 with tempo gained elsewhere. 4...Nf6 5.Qe2 is the second
option and the move order most players use. There 5.Nc3 is the Mad Dog of the 3.Nc3 chapter.

**Trap:** 4...Nd7?? 5.Bxf7+ Kxf7 6.Ng5+ and the king is dragged out. The same pattern punishes ...Bg4
when the bishop on c4 and the knight on f3 can combine on f7.

## Against 3.f4: ...c5 before the Austrian
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. f4 c5
arrows: Gc5d4 Gg7d4
squares: Yd4

After 3.f4 d6 4.Nf3 Nf6 5.Nc3 White has the Austrian Attack: the whole centre, with ...c5 as Black's only
counterplay. 3...c5 takes that counterplay at once while d4 has only the queen and the knight to come.
- **4.d5** gives a Benoni (next section).
- **4.c3 cxd4 5.cxd4 d5 6.e5 Nc6** and d4 falls: the knights and ...Qb6 attack it, and ...Bg4xf3 removes its
  last defender.
- **4.dxc5 Qa5+** takes the pawn back with a check.

3...d5 4.e5 c5 is the second option, with ...f6 to open the bishop's diagonal. If you play 3...d6 4.Nf3 Nf6
anyway, 5.Nc3 is the Austrian of the 3.Nc3 chapter. 5.Bd3 O-O 6.O-O c5 7.d5 e6 reaches the same Benoni.

## The Benoni after 3.f4 c5 4.d5
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. f4 c5 4. d5 d6 5. Nf3 Nf6 6. Nc3 O-O 7. Bd3 e6 8. O-O exd5 9. exd5
arrows: Gb8a6 Ga6c7 Rf3d2 Rd2c4 Rf4f5
squares: Rd6

After the exchange on d5 White's e-pawn is gone, and the e-file and e4 belong to Black. The pawn on f4 is not
attacking anything: it only blocks the bishop on c1. Black's knight goes a6-c7 to hit d5, and the queenside
majority with ...a6, ...Rb8 and ...b5 follows. The weakness is d6. White aims a knight at it from c4 (via d2)
or b5, and wants f4-f5 against the king. The two studies disagree on who is better. The plans are clear for
both sides.

## 3.c3 and the Lazy Variation
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. c3 d6 4. Bg5 Nf6 5. Nd2 O-O 6. Ngf3 h6 7. Bh4 Qe8 8. Be2 e5
arrows: Gf6h5 Gh5f4
squares: Yf4

3.c3 makes a fortress of d4 and leaves White's set-up open. With 4.Nf3 and 5.Bd3 it is the Bd3 structure
above. With 4.Bg5 and 5.Nd2 (the Lazy Variation) Black castles, asks the bishop with ...h6 and breaks with
...e5. The study's rule of thumb: break with ...c5 when White can still castle long, and with ...e5 when
he cannot. After the exchange on e5 the knight goes h5-f4. Against 4.f4, the sources hit at once with
4...c5 5.dxc5 Nf6. The pawn comes back and the queen trade after e5 leaves Black's king safe on d8.

## 3.Be3: castle only after the bishops come off
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. Nf3 d6 4. Be3 Nf6 5. Nc3 c6 6. Qd2 b5
arrows: Gb5b4 Gc6c5
squares: Rh6

Be3 with Qd2 is the 150 Attack set-up, and Bh6 is the threat. 3.Be3 d6 4.Nc3 and 4.Qd2 transpose to the 3.Nc3
chapter. With Nf3 or Bd3 instead, the sources choose the Czech set-up: ...c6, ...b5, ...Nbd7 and ...Qc7, with
castling put off until White has traded on g7 with Bh6. Then ...b4 drives the knight away and ...c5 opens
the queenside.

## 3.c4: the King's Indian, or ...e5 at once
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. c4 d6 4. Nc3 e5 5. d5 a5
arrows: Gb8a6 Ga6c5 Gf7f5
squares: Yc5

Against the third central pawn the Modern's queenside plans (...a6, ...b5) are out. 4.Nc3 Nf6 is the King's
Indian proper (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6), and its chapters take over. 4...e5 is the way to stay
outside them. After d5, ...a5 stops b4, the knight goes a6-c5, and with the g8-knight still at home ...f5
needs no preparation. 3...Nc6 hitting d4 at once is a sharper second option from one study.

## Wing pawns and early queens: strike in the centre
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. d4 Bg7 3. h4 d5 4. e5 c5 5. c3 Nc6
arrows: Gc6d4 Gc5d4 Gf7f6

Against h4 the answer is the centre, not the wing. ...d5 and ...c5 attack d4, ...f6 hits e5, and if White
pushes h5-h6 the knight sacrifice ...Nxh6 wins the pawn back with a sound position (6.h5 cxd4 7.cxd4 Qb6
8.h6 Nxh6). 2.h4 is met the same way, with 2...d5.

Against the queen on f3 (2.Bc4 Bg7 3.Qf3 or 3.Bc4 d6 4.Qf3), ...e6 shuts the diagonal to f7. After 2.Bc4 Bg7
3.Qf3 e6 4.d4 Black simply takes on d4. In the Bishop Attack, ...e6 leaves dark holes that ...e5 repairs
as soon as White has castled.

## Move orders
moves: 1. e4 g6 2. Nf3 Bg7 3. d4 d6
arrows: Gb1c3

Most of White's second moves come back to positions covered elsewhere. The moves to keep straight:
- 2.Nf3 Bg7 3.d4 d6 is 3.Nf3 d6. 2.f4 Bg7 3.Nf3 d6 4.d4 is 3.f4 d6 4.Nf3.
- 2.Nc3 Bg7 3.Nf3 d6 4.d4 and 3.Nf3 d6 4.Nc3 are the 3.Nc3 chapter's 4.Nf3. 4.Be2 Nf6 5.Nc3 is its Classical.
- 2.Bc4 Bg7 3.Nf3 d6 4.d4 is the Bishop Attack with Nf3, the 4.Bc4 lines above.
- 2...d6 instead of 2...Bg7 is mostly the same, but 3.Nc3 Nf6 is a Pirc. Play 3...Bg7 to stay in the Modern.
- Without d4, 2.Nc3 Bg7 3.g3 lets Black play ...e5 and ...c5 to clamp d4. Against 3.f4, 3...a6 4.a4 c5
  is a Sicilian with ...g6 already in.

## What is still a draft
- **3.e5 has no published source.** The line stops where the elite games stop. Against 4.f4 the plan is
  ...c5 and ...dxe5, but nothing covers it move by move.
- **3.f4 d6 4.Nf3 Nf6 5.Bd3 O-O 6.O-O** (the ...d6 order) has no source line. The file reaches the Benoni with
  ...c5 and ...e6. 7.dxc5 and 7.c3 are open.
- **3.Be3 d6 4.Bd3 Nf6:** only 5.Nc3 c6 is sourced. 5.Nf3, 5.c3, 5.Nd2 and 5.e5 are not.
- **4.Bd3 Nf6 5.O-O O-O 6.Nbd2 and 6.Nc3** have no source line. 4.Bc4 and 4.Be3 rest on one study each.
- **Not covered:** 4.Nbd2, 4.Bf4, 2.Nf3 Bg7 3.d3, 2.Nc3 Bg7 3.d3 and 3.Bc4, 3.c4 d6 4.Be3, 2.c4, 2.d3, 3.Bd3.
- The first books to check, if a copy can be read: Hillarp Persson, *The Modern Tiger* (chapters "Lazy
  Variation with c2-c3", "Unusual Lines", "Averbakh Variation") and Lakdawala, *Opening Repertoire: The
  Modern Defence* ("Other Tries for White").
