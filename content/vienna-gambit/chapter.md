With 3.f4 White plays a King's Gambit in which Black's knight is already on f6. That one detail changes
everything: taking on f4 is bad because e4-e5 comes with tempo, and every quiet third move lets White play
fxe5 followed by d4 and e5. Only 3...d5 holds the balance, and after 4.fxe5 Nxe4 the real opening begins:
White has a pawn wedge on e5 and a half-open f-file, Black has a strong knight on e4 and free development.

Theory's verdict is that Black equalises with accurate play, and the sources agree on where the accuracy is
needed. The classical 5.Nf3 is under a cloud because of 5...Bc5 (Finkel's survey in Yearbook 110, Tseitlin
and Glazkov before him). 5.d3 is met by 5...Nxc3 6.bxc3 d4. That leaves Spielmann's 5.Qf3, which Goeller's
survey of the literature, Breutigam's ChessBase Magazine article, the Sveshnikovs' repertoire book and
Illingworth's guide all single out as White's best practical try. It is the main repertoire here; 5.Nf3
and 5.d3 follow as complete second weapons, because the traps and the plans in them are worth knowing even
if you never switch.

The lines are short on forced wins and long on patterns. Learn which pawn takes back on c3 and why, what
the queen does on e2, f3 and g3, and how the queenless ending is played, and most positions in the chapter
can be handled by understanding.

## The position after 4...Nxe4
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4
arrows: Gd1f3 Bg1f3 Bd2d3 Rd8h4
squares: Ye4

Black threatens ...Qh4+ and the knight on e4 stops White from developing normally (d4 would leave it a
permanent outpost, Bc4 is not possible). White has to question the knight at once, and there are three
ways.

- **5.Qf3** attacks it a second time and defuses the check: 5...Qh4+ 6.g3 Nxg3 7.Qxg3. The g1 knight goes
  to e2 later. Black chooses between 5...Nc6 (the forcing main line), 5...Nxc3 and 5...f5.
- **5.Nf3** develops and protects e5; the knight on e4 is then attacked by Qe2 or d3. Black's replies are
  5...Be7, 5...Bc5, 5...Bg4, 5...Nc6 and 5...Bb4.
- **5.d3** hits it with a pawn and invites 5...Qh4+ and 5...Bb4, both of which are worse than they look.

5.Nxe4 is the move to avoid: after 5...dxe4 the pawn takes f3 from the knight, ...Qh4+ is still there and
e5 becomes a target.

## The e5 wedge and the half-open f-file
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Qf3 Nxc3 6. dxc3 Be6 7. Bf4 Be7 8. O-O-O
arrows: Gf3g3 Gh2h4 Rf7f6 Rc7c5
squares: Ye5 Rd5

Once the knights are exchanged on c3 the structure decides the plans. The e5 pawn takes f6 and d6 from
Black's pieces, so the black king has few defenders, and it gives White's pieces the squares f4 and (after
an exchange on d4 or e6) d4. The f-file points at f7.

- **White** castles long, puts the bishop on f4 and the queen on g3, and pushes the h-pawn. The standard
  attacking model is Paulsen-Schiffers 1889: Qg3, Bd3, Nf3-g5, h4 and the h-file opens.
- **Black** has two pawn breaks. ...f6 removes the wedge and is the equalising idea in most lines, so
  White wants to meet it with a piece on e5 or with the pin on the d-file. ...c5 gains space and prepares
  ...Nc6 and ...Qa5; White answers with c4, using the rook on d1 against d5.
- **Trades:** Black wants the f4 bishop off (it holds e5), which is the point of ...Bg5. White is happy to
  give the light-squared bishop for the c6 knight, because that knight is the piece that attacks e5.

## Which pawn takes back on c3
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Qf3 Nc6 6. Bb5 Nxc3 7. dxc3
arrows: Gc1f4 Ge1c1 Gd1d5
squares: Yc3

The recapture is the most important decision in the opening, and the rule is simple.

- **With the d-pawn** when it is still on d2: the c1 bishop and the a1 rook come out at once, the d-file
  bears on d5 and White castles long. This is Paulsen's recapture in the 5.Qf3 lines and the point of the
  Qe2 systems after 5.Nf3. In the main line 7.bxc3 is a known inaccuracy: the same ending arises with
  White's bishop shut in and Black slightly better.
- **With the b-pawn** when d3 has been played (5.Qf3 f5 6.d3, or 5.d3). Then White wants d3-d4 and the
  chain c3-d4-e5, and Black's standard antidote is ...d4 himself, giving a pawn back to stop it.
- **One exception:** after 5.Nf3 Bc5 6.Qe2 Bf2+ 7.Kd1 Nxc3+ White takes with the b-pawn, because 8.dxc3
  lets the f2 bishop out via h4 with tempo.

## The queenless ending
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Qf3 Nc6 6. Bb5 Nxc3 7. dxc3 Qh4+ 8. g3 Qe4+ 9. Qxe4 dxe4 10. Bxc6+ bxc6 11. Be3
arrows: Gg1e2 Ge2d4 Ge1c1 Gh1e1
squares: Re4 Rc6 Rc7 Ye5

About four in ten of the 5.Qf3 Nc6 games reach this ending, so it has to be played well. Black has the bishop
pair; White has the healthier pawns and a knight that will sit on d4.

- **White's plan:** Be3 first (so that ...Bc5 does not take the g1-a7 diagonal), then Ne2-d4, O-O-O and a
  rook to e1 or f1. The targets are e4, which has lost the d-pawn that supported it, and the c-pawns. The
  f-file is useful: Rf1 often wins f7 or forces a concession.
- **Black's plan:** attack e5 with a rook (...Rb8-b5, ...Ra5 or ...Re8), open the h-file with ...h5-h4,
  and exchange a bishop for the d4 knight when it suits him.
- **Good trades for White:** the e-pawns (e5 for e4) and one pair of rooks. In the model game
  Lagarde-Bouget, White let e5 go, took e4 and was left with the better structure and a dominant knight.
- **Verdict:** equal with best play. Illingworth's description is the practical one: an equal position
  that the stronger player wins.

## Black keeps the knight: 5...f5
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Qf3 f5 6. d3 Nxc3 7. bxc3 d4 8. Qg3
arrows: Gf1e2 Ge2h5 Ge2f3 Gg1e2
squares: Rg7 Rd4

The Bardeleben Variation is the line that makes 5.Qf3 a practical weapon and not a refutation. Black
supports the knight, and after 6.d3 Nxc3 7.bxc3 plays 7...d4, the same pawn offer as in the 5.d3 line, so
that White never gets d3-d4.

White's answer is 8.Qg3 (Bangiev). The queen ties the f8 bishop to g7 and frees f3 and e2: Be2 comes with
the threat of Bh5+, then Bf3 and Ne2. White gives up the c3 pawn and gets the bishops, the long light
diagonal and castling; with 12.Bxc6 he removes the knight that could use b4 and d4 and has lasting
compensation. Illingworth, who recommends 5.Qf3, rates this line as objectively equal and the one he
would be least happy to face in a slow game, so it is the line to know best.

## The queen on e2 in the 5.Nf3 lines
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Nf3 Be7 6. Qe2
arrows: Ge2b5 Gd2d3 Gc1f4 Ge1c1
squares: Ye4

"If in doubt, play Qe2" is Gary Lane's summary of White's play after 5.Nf3. The move does four things: it
attacks the knight on e4 again and prepares d3, it lets White take back on c3 with the d-pawn and castle
long, it sets up Qb5+ against an undefended b7 (a recurring trick after ...Bg4), and it keeps d2-d4 in
reserve so that ...Bb4 has no target.

It works against 5...Be7 (Hector's and Adams's line), 5...Bg4 (Kaufmann's move, with two famous traps),
5...Bb4 and, after 6.Bb5 first, against 5...Nc6. Against 5...Bc5 it is White's best try as well, but there
6...Bf5 followed by ...d4 is the line that the surveys consider better for Black, which is why 5.Nf3 is
the second choice in this chapter.

## Traps and standard tactics
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 d5 4. fxe5 Nxe4 5. Qf3 Nc6 6. Nxe4 Nd4
arrows: Rd4f3 Rd4c2 Rd5e4
squares: Rc2

- **6.Nxe4? Nd4!** (diagram). After 5.Qf3 Nc6 White must pin with 6.Bb5. Taking on e4 loses to the knight
  jump: 7.Qd3 dxe4 8.Qxe4 Bf5 and c2 falls with check.
- **The Würzburger Trap:** 5.d3 Qh4+? 6.g3 Nxg3 7.Nf3 Qh5 8.Nxd5!. If 8...Nxh1 then 9.Nxc7+ and 10.Nxa8,
  and the h1 knight does not get out. After 8...Bg4 White plays 9.Bg2, or goes for the old finish
  9.Nf4 Bxf3 10.Nxh5 Bxd1 11.hxg3 Bxc2? 12.b3, trapping the bishop.
- **Euwe-Yates:** 5.Nf3 Bg4 6.Qe2 Nc6? 7.Nxe4 Nd4 8.Qd3 Bxf3 9.Ng3!. The quiet retreat leaves two black
  pieces attacked.
- **Spielmann-Flamberg:** 5.Nf3 Bg4 6.Qe2 Nc5?! 7.d4 Bxf3 8.Qxf3 Qh4+ 9.g3 Qxd4 10.Be3 Qxe5 11.O-O-O and
  12.Nxd5!. Black's queen has eaten two pawns and the d-file opens on his king.
- **The pin on the d-file:** with a rook on d1 against a queen on d8, c4 attacks a d5 pawn that cannot
  take, and ...f6 fxe5 can be met by Rxd5 or even Qxe4 and Bc4+ (the 5...Bb4 line).
- **Bh5+ in the 5...f5 line:** once Black has played ...f5 the diagonal e8-h5 is weak. With the queen on
  g3 a bishop check on h5 forces ...g6 and a sacrifice on g6 follows.

## When Black does not play 3...d5
moves: 1. e4 e5 2. Nc3 Nf6 3. f4 exf4 4. e5
arrows: Ge5f6 Gc3d5 Gd1h5
squares: Rf6 Rg8

Most games below master level leave the main line on move three, and the punishment is always the same
pawn move. After 3...exf4 4.e5 the knight has no square but g8 (e4 and d5 are covered by the c3 knight,
g4 and h5 by the queen), and the attempt to pin with 4...Qe7 fails to 5.Qe2 and Nd5. After 3...Nc6
4.fxe5 Nxe5 5.d4 both knights are driven back in turn. After 3...Bb4 or 3...Bd6 White takes on e5 and
wins material with Qg4 or with the fork e4-e5.

The only respectable alternative is 3...d6, which gives a King's Gambit Declined without the active
bishop on c5. White keeps the tension with Nf3 and Bb5 and has a small, risk-free advantage.
