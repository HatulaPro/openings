The Classical is the main road of the King's Indian. White develops without committing anything, and after
6...e5 he has to decide what to do with the centre. The repertoire answers 7.O-O with 7...Nc6, which forces
that decision at once: after 8.d5 Ne7 the centre is closed and the game becomes the Mar del Plata, a race
between White's queenside (c4-c5, the c-file, the squares c7 and d6) and Black's kingside (...f5-f4, ...g5-g4).

White is usually faster and Black plays for higher stakes. That is the whole character of the line: Black
has to spend exactly as many moves on defence as the position demands and not one more, and he has to know
the standard regrouping and the standard sacrifices by heart, because they are too slow to work out at the board.

The chapter also covers the ways White avoids the race: the Bayonet (9.b4), which fights on both wings, the
Petrosian (7.d5), the Gligoric (7.Be3) and the Exchange (7.dxe5). Against all of them the same pieces go to
the same kinds of squares, and the notes say where the differences lie.

## Why 7...Nc6, and the other seventh moves
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6
arrows: Rc6d4 Re5d4 Bd4d5
squares: Yd4

7...Nc6 attacks d4 a second time, so White cannot go on making useful moves and keep the tension. 8.d5 gains a tempo on the knight, but it also gives Black what he
wants: a fixed centre, a clear plan and a king's knight that can leave f6 without e4 becoming a target.
8.Be3 and 8.dxe5 are the only ways to avoid this and both are harmless.

The other seventh moves are respectable and are not the repertoire:

- **7...Na6** keeps everything flexible (the knight goes to c5 after d5) and avoids the heaviest theory,
  but it puts no pressure on d4, so White chooses the structure.
- **7...Nbd7** is the old main line: solid, with ...c6, ...Re8 and ...exd4 in reserve. It blocks the
  c8-bishop and leaves White a free hand with Re1, Bf1 and Rb1.
- **7...exd4** 8.Nxd4 Re8 9.f3 gives up the centre for piece play against e4 and d4. White keeps more space
  and a small pull if he knows what he is doing.

## The Mar del Plata structure
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7
arrows: Gf7f5 Gg6g5 Rc4c5 Rb2b4
squares: Yd6 Yc7 Rg2

The pawn chains point in opposite directions and each side attacks the base of the other's chain. White
wants c4-c5 and cxd6, then the c-file, a knight on b5 and an entry on c7; the pawn on d6 and the squares c7
and a7 are his targets. Black wants ...f5-f4, ...g5-g4 and then ...g3 or ...gxf3, and his target is g2 and
the king behind it.

Three things decide the race:

- **The f6-knight must get out of the f-pawn's way.** After 9.Ne1 it goes to d7, where it also holds up
  c5; after 9.b4 it goes to h5, because c5 can no longer be held up and the knight is better aimed at f4.
- **Black never plays ...fxe4.** It gives White the e4-square and the open diagonal for nothing. The pawn
  goes to f4 as soon as White has played f3 or Be3.
- **White's dark-squared bishop decides which line it is.** On e3-f2 it eyes a7 and defends the king but
  loses a tempo to ...f4; on d2 it supports the queenside from a distance and keeps e1 for retreat.

## The attacking set-up
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7 9. Ne1 Nd7 10. Nd3 f5 11. Bd2 Nf6 12. f3 f4 13. c5 g5 14. Rc1 Ng6 15. cxd6 cxd6 16. Nb5 Rf7 17. Qc2 Ne8 18. a4 h5 19. Nf2 Bf8 20. h3 Rg7
arrows: Gg5g4 Gg6h4 Ge8f6 Gc8h3
squares: Yc7 Yd6

This is the formation Gligoric worked out, and every piece in it does two jobs:

- **...Rf7** covers c7 along the rank, so that the c-file leads nowhere, and frees f8.
- **...Bf8** covers d6, the base of the chain, and frees g7.
- **...Rg7** puts the rook behind the g-pawn, where ...g4 opens its file.
- **...Ng6** and then **...Nh4** bring the knight to the square that attacks g2 and f3.
- **...h5** supports ...g4. When White has played h3, ...g4 comes as a pawn sacrifice and the knight
  returns from e8 to f6 to recapture or to jump to h5 and g3.
- **...Ne8** is the one purely defensive move: it guards c7 and d6 while the others are regrouping, and it
  comes back to f6 as soon as the queenside is under control.

The order depends on what White threatens. A knight on b5 means ...Rf7 at once; pressure on d6 means
...Bf8; if White is slow, ...h5 and ...g4 come first.

## The c8-bishop and the sacrifices
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7 9. Ne1 Nd7 10. Be3 f5 11. f3 f4 12. Bf2 g5 13. Nd3 Nf6 14. c5 Ng6 15. Rc1 Rf7 16. Rc2 Bf8 17. cxd6 cxd6 18. Qd2 g4 19. Rfc1 g3
arrows: Gf6h5 Gd8h4 Gc8h3
squares: Rh2 Rg2

The bishop on c8 looks undeveloped for twenty moves and is the most important attacking piece on the board.
From its original square it supports ...g4 and it is the piece that sacrifices itself on h3 when White has
answered ...g3 or ...g4 with h3. Black therefore avoids exchanging it: he does not put it on d7 unless that
gains time against a knight on b5, he answers Bg4 ideas by keeping the diagonal closed, and in
Korchnoi-Kasparov he even played ...Bb7-c8 to bring it home. White, for his part, will give a lot to
exchange it, with Nb5-c7-e6 or Na7xc8.

The standard blows:

- **...g3** as a pawn sacrifice (the diagram, from Taimanov-Najdorf, 1953): after hxg3 fxg3 Bxg3 Black has
  ...Nh5, ...Bh6 or ...Be7-g5, the f4-square and the dark squares around the king. If White ignores the pawn
  it sits on g3 and the queen comes to h4.
- **...Bxh3** when a pawn stands on g3 and White has played h3: gxh3 Qd7 or ...Qc8 and the queen arrives on
  h3. Kotronias's rule of thumb: once a pawn has reached g3, the d8-h4 diagonal is what matters, and
  clearing it by sacrifice is often correct.
- **...g4 with h3 already played**: fxg4 hxg4 hxg4 (or Nxg4) and Black uses the h-file, h4 for the knight
  and g5 for the bishop.
- **...Nxg3** in the 9.Nd2 lines, where White has played g3 against a knight on h5.

## Holding the queenside
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7 9. Ne1 Nd7 10. Be3 f5 11. f3 f4 12. Bf2 g5 13. Rc1 Ng6 14. c5 Nxc5 15. b4 Na6
arrows: Rc3b5 Rf2a7 Gc8d7
squares: Yc7 Ra7

Black cannot stop c5; he can only make it cost something. With the knight on d7, c5 needs b4 or Nd3 first,
and in the modern main line (the diagram) White gives the pawn up to save the time. Black takes it.

The rules for the defence:

- **Nb5 aims at c7 and at a7.** Against c7 the answer is ...Rf7, never a panicky ...Ne8 unless d6 needs it
  as well. Against a7, with White's bishop on f2, the answer is ...b6 and not ...a6, which runs into Na7!
  and the loss of the c8-bishop.
- **...a6** is right when the knight simply has to go back (with the bishop on d2, or after a4) and the
  hole on b6 cannot be used.
- **After cxd6, recapture with the c-pawn.** The chain stays intact and the c-file is covered by ...Rf7.
- **A pawn on a7 can be left hanging** when the knight that takes it is stranded there, or when ...Rc7
  hits a queen on c2. Count the tempi: a knight on a7 is three moves from the kingside.
- **Do not defend more than once.** Every extra defensive move is a tempo taken from ...h5 and ...g4.

## The Bayonet: the knight on e6
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7 9. b4 Nh5 10. Re1 f5 11. Ng5 Nf6
arrows: Rg5e6 Gc7c6 Gh7h6
squares: Ye6

9.b4 prepares c5 in one move and keeps the king's knight on f3, from where it can go to g5 and e6 the
moment Black plays ...f5. That changes the game: the race is replaced by a fight for the centre.

Black's knight goes to h5 instead of d7 because holding up c5 is no longer possible and f4 is a real
outpost. White meets that with Re1 (so that ...Nf4 can be answered by Bf1) and after ...f5 with Ng5. The
knight cannot be tolerated on e6, so Black takes it, and a white pawn appears on e6. It cuts Black's
position in two, and it is also far from home: Black surrounds it with ...c6, ...Qc8 or ...Nc6-d4 and
opens the centre with ...d5 when the long diagonal and the f-file favour him. Most main lines end in
equal positions with opposite plans rather than in mating attacks, and Black needs patience here more than
memory.

## 9.Nd2: prophylaxis on both sides
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7 9. Nd2 a5 10. a3 Nd7 11. Rb1 f5 12. b4 Kh8
arrows: Ge7g8 Gg8f6 Gf6h5
squares: Yc5

With the knight on d2 White is a move faster with c5 and Nc4 than after 9.Ne1, so the straightforward race
is bad for Black. He slows White down with ...a5, which costs White a3 and Rb1, and then plays the
manoeuvre that belongs to this line: ...Kh8 and ...Ng8. The knight on e7 has no future on g6 here, because
White has not played f3 and f4 is not yet a target; from g8 it goes to f6 or h6, the bishop gets h6, and
the king is off the a2-g8 diagonal. White's knight on d2 is passive on the kingside, so Black can afford
to build up slowly and sacrifice on g3 when the pieces are ready.

## The Petrosian System
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. d5 a5 8. Bg5 h6 9. Bh4 Na6 10. Nd2 Qe8 11. O-O Nh7 12. a3 Bd7 13. b3 h5 14. f3 Bh6
arrows: Gh6e3 Ga6c5 Gf7f5
squares: Ye3 Yc5

White closes the centre before Black's knight is on c6, so there is no ...Ne7, and he pins the f6-knight to
make ...f5 hard to arrange. Black's set-up is a chain of small moves, each with a precise point: ...a5
secures c5 for a knight; ...Na6 rather than ...Nbd7 keeps d7 free; ...h6 fixes the bishop's diagonal;
...Qe8 breaks the pin; ...Nh7 clears the f-pawn; ...Bd7 connects queen and rook so that b4 loses a pawn;
...h5 threatens to trap the bishop with ...g5 and ...h4 and so forces f3; and then ...Bh6-e3 exchanges or
activates the bad bishop through the hole f3 has left. Only after that does ...f5 come.

## The Gligoric System and the bishop dance
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. Be3 Ng4 8. Bg5 f6
arrows: Bg5h4 Bg5c1 Gb8c6 Gg4h6

7.Be3 keeps the king in the centre and the tension in place, and it takes the sting out of 7...Nc6: after
8.d5 Ne7 9.Nd2! White has not castled into the attack and is ready for the queenside. The repertoire
therefore hits the bishop with 7...Ng4 8.Bg5 f6. Both sides have made concessions: White's bishop has moved
three times, and Black has blocked his own bishop with ...f6 and has a knight to bring back via h6 and f7.
Now ...Nc6 is good, because d5 no longer comes with Nd2 in hand. The same positions arise from 7.O-O Nc6
8.Be3 Ng4, and the lines are built to transpose.

## The Exchange Variation
moves: 1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. dxe5 dxe5 8. Qxd8 Rxd8 9. Bg5 Re8
arrows: Rc3d5 Gc7c6 Gb8a6
squares: Yd4 Rd5

White wins nothing by force (9.Nxe5 Nxe4), and the structure favours Black in the long run: he can cover
d5 with ...c6, while d4 is a permanent hole in White's camp. White's chances lie in his lead in
development over the next five moves, with Bg5, Nd5 and O-O-O. Black answers each threat exactly, exchanges
the knight that lands on d5, plays ...c6, and then manoeuvres a knight towards d4 by ...Na6-c5-e6 or
...Nd7-c5-e6. The ending is equal, and it is the player who knows these manoeuvres who wins it.
