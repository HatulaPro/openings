The Advance is the principled answer to the Caro-Kann: White takes the space on offer and keeps every
piece on the board. The price is that Black gets what the French player never gets, the queen's bishop
outside the pawn chain. The whole opening is an argument about whether that bishop on f5 is an asset
or a target, and whether Black can break with ...c5 or ...f6 before the lack of space tells.

The repertoire has three parts. Against 3...Bf5 the main weapon is the **Short System**, 4.Nf3 e6
5.Be2: quiet development, then c4 or dxc5 to open the centre the moment Black plays ...c5 with the
king still on e8. The second weapon, in its own lines, is the **Tal Variation** 4.h4, which goes
straight for the bishop and gives sharper, less symmetrical play. Against 3...c5 White takes, 4.dxc5,
and makes Black spend time winning the pawn back.

Be honest about what it asks for. The forcing lines after 5...c5 6.Be3 are deeply analysed, and a
fully prepared opponent can steer the 6...Qb6 line into a drawn ending. Everyone else gets a hard
defensive task.

## Choosing between the Advance, the Fantasy and the Exchange
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5
arrows: Rc8f5 Rc6c5
squares: Yd4 Ye5

- **The Advance** suits a player who is happy to sit on a space advantage, manoeuvre on both wings and
  wait for the right moment to open the game. Nothing is exchanged early, so the stronger player has
  time to outplay the weaker one. It needs the most theory of the three, almost all of it in the 5...c5
  lines.
- **The Fantasy** (3.f3) is the choice when you want an open fight and a surprised opponent at once,
  and accept some structural risk for it.
- **The Exchange with 4.Bd3** gives a healthy, risk-free game with a clear plan, but Black is never in
  danger of being worse out of the opening.

If the complaint about the old Exchange lines was "equal and nothing to play for", the Advance is the
direct cure: the pawn on e5 takes f6 from Black's knight, the kingside pieces have to queue for e7,
and Black cannot castle quickly.

## The structure: a French with the bishop outside
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2
arrows: Rc6c5 Rf7f6 Gc2c4 Gf3h4
squares: Rf5 Yd4

Black has the French pawn chain without the French bishop. Three things are paid for it.

- **A tempo.** The c-pawn reaches c5 in two moves. That is why White can afford the modest Be2 and
  still be ahead in development when the centre opens.
- **The queenside light squares.** With the bishop on f5 nobody guards b7, and once the c-pawn moves,
  b5, c6 and the a4-e8 diagonal are soft. Bb5, Qb3, Qa4 and Nb5 keep turning up for that reason.
- **The bishop itself is a target.** Nh4 wins the bishop pair if the bishop has no retreat, and g4
  gains time when the centre is closed.

Black's breaks are ...c5 against d4 and ...f6 against e5. White's levers are c4, opening the centre
before Black has castled, and on the kingside f4-f5 after Ne1 or Nh4.

## Short's idea: develop, then punish ...c5
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2 c5 6. Be3
arrows: Gd4c5 Gc2c4 Rd8b6
squares: Rb2 Yd4

Nigel Short's discovery was that White does not need to chase the bishop. Black's kingside is hard
to develop (the knight wants e7, the bishop wants e7, f6 is unavailable), so ...c5 always comes with
the king in the centre.

- **6.Be3, not 6.c3.** Supporting d4 with the pawn gives a French where Black's extra piece on f5
  outweighs the lost tempo. The bishop on e3 lets White recapture on d4 with a knight, which then hits
  f5 and b5, and it prepares dxc5.
- **When to take on c5.** Take when Black cannot recapture at once with the bishop: when a knight on
  e7 blocks it, or when ...Nc6 has been played against a bishop on e3 (then ...Bxc5 runs into Bxc5,
  ...Qa5+ and b4, winning a piece). White then holds the pawn with Be3 and b4 for as long as it is
  convenient and gives it back for a lead in development.
- **When to play c4.** Play it when Black's king is still on e8 and a knight on e7 or d7 blocks the
  f8-bishop. After ...dxc4 a knight recaptures (Na3 or Nd2) and looks at d6.

## The b2-pawn
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2 c5 6. Be3 Qb6 7. Nc3 Nc6 8. O-O Qxb2 9. Qe1
arrows: Ga1b1 Gc3b5 Rb2c3
squares: Rb2 Yc7

After 6...Qb6 the b-pawn cannot be defended comfortably, so White gives it up twice, on move 7 and
again on move 8. The compensation is concrete: the queen on b2 is short of squares and the knight
jumps to b5 with threats on c7.

- If Black takes at once, 7...Qxb2, then 8.Qb1 forces the queens off and the rook lands on b7.
- In the main line the knight on c3 hangs, and 9.Qe1 is the move: it defends the knight so that Rb1
  becomes possible (9.Rb1 would simply lose the knight).
- The standard punishment for a careless move is Rb1, Nb5 and Nc7+, winning the rook on a8. It works
  after 9...c4 because the queen has to go to a3 and a4.

## The slow set-ups: Nbd2-b3 and play on both wings
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2 Nd7 6. O-O Ne7 7. Nbd2 h6 8. Nb3
arrows: Ga2a4 Gf3e1 Ge1d3 Gf2f4 Rg7g5 Rf7f6
squares: Yc5

When Black holds back ...c5 with ...Nd7, ...Ne7 and ...h6 in some order, White builds the same set-up
every time. The knight on b3 controls c5 and a5, so ...c5 costs a pawn or hands White the d4-square.
Then:

- **Queenside:** a4-a5 gains space and fixes b7; if Black stops it with ...a5, the pawn on a5 is a
  target for Bd2 and b5 belongs to White.
- **Kingside:** Ne1-d3 frees the f-pawn; f4, and g4 once the bishop has gone to h7, start the pawn
  storm.
- **Centre:** c4 is held back until Black commits the king.

Black's counterplay is ...g5 with ...Bg7 and ...f6, or ...Nf5 and ...Be7 with a late ...c5. White's
king is the safer one, and the bishop on h7 can end up shut out by its own pawns.

## Good and bad trades
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. Nf3 e6 5. Be2 c5 6. Be3 cxd4 7. Nxd4 Ne7 8. O-O Nbc6 9. Bb5
arrows: Gb5c6 Gd4f5
squares: Rd5 Rc6

- **Light-squared bishops:** in the Short System do not offer the trade with Bd3. Black's bishop is
  the piece that is short of a future; White's is the one that will use the weak light squares.
- **Knight for the f5-bishop:** Nxf5 is good when Black must retake with the e-pawn. The pawns on d5
  and f5 are then fixed, d4 is a permanent square for a White piece, and White has the bishops. It is
  less good when a knight recaptures and sits on f5 attacking e3 and d4.
- **The double exchange** in the diagram, Bxc6+ and Nxf5, breaks both of Black's pawn groups before
  c4 opens the centre.
- **Dark-squared bishops:** their exchange suits White, because d6 and c5 lose their defender.
- **Endings:** space and the bishop pair make most of them pleasant, with one exception to know: the
  pawn-down ending of the 6...Qb6 main line, which is level.

## The second weapon: the Tal Variation 4.h4
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5 4. h4 h5 5. Bd3 Bxd3 6. Qxd3 e6 7. Bg5
arrows: Gb1d2 Gc2c4
squares: Rh5 Yg5

4.h4 threatens to trap the bishop (4...e6 loses it to g4, f3 and h5), so Black has to move the
h-pawn. After the usual 4...h5 two things have changed for good: g5 is an outpost that no pawn can
attack, and h5 is a weakness. That turns the normally harmless exchange Bd3 into a serious plan. The
queen recaptures on d3, the bishop goes to g5 where it paralyses the kingside, and c4 opens the game.

After 4...h6 White plays g4 and Black must choose between a passive retreat to d7 and allowing the
pawn sacrifice e6, which shuts in the f8-bishop.

Compared with the Short System the positions are less worked out to a draw, and White's own king is
a little airier.

## 3...c5: why it is not a French a tempo down
moves: 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 c5 4. dxc5 e6 5. a3
arrows: Gb2b4 Gc1b2 Rf8c5
squares: Ye5

Black attacks d4 at once and keeps the bishop at home, hoping to bring it out to g4 later. Against
the French recipe, 4.c3, that works well: Black has lost a move but has the good bishop. So White
changes the structure with 4.dxc5.

The tempo count now favours White. Black needs ...e6 and ...Bxc5 to regain the pawn, the bishop on
c8 is shut in after all, and 5.a3 prepares b4 with a further gain of time. White's set-up is b4, Bb2,
Nf3, Bd3 and castles, with the e5-pawn overprotected and c4 in reserve. Black plays against e5 with
...Ne7-g6 and ...f6, and against the queenside pawns with ...a5.
